00 The whole map

A "distance" answers one question: how far apart are these two things? The catch is that "far" depends entirely on what the numbers mean. Pick from the wrong family and the maths still runs — it just answers a question you never asked.

Five families, chosen by what your data is
Your data looks like…FamilyDefault pickBecause
Continuous features on comparable scales (height, weight, price)Minkowski EuclideanStraight-line intuition, works with k-means/KNN out of the box

Sets: tokens, q-grams, tags, SKUs seen togetherSet overlap JaccardOnly shared membership matters; order and counts do not

Key idea. Distance and similarity are two views of one number. similarity = 1 − distance holds only when the distance is bounded to [0, 1] — true for cosine, Jaccard and normalised edit ratios, false for raw Euclidean or Levenshtein counts. Most "why is my threshold weird?" bugs are somebody mixing the two.

01 What makes a "metric" and why you should care

Anything can be called a distance function. A metric is a distance that obeys four rules. The rules are not academic trivia: index structures, clustering guarantees and pruning tricks all quietly assume them.

1. Non-negativity d(x, y) ≥ 0 2. Identity d(x, y) = 0 ⟺ x = y 3. Symmetry d(x, y) = d(y, x) 4. Triangle inequality d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z) ← the one that gets broken
The triangle inequality, and what breaking it costs you

🎯 Why it matters concretely
  • ANN indexes (FAISS, HNSW, Annoy) prune with the triangle inequality; feed them a non-metric and recall silently drops.
  • Hierarchical clustering with Ward linkage assumes squared Euclidean — swapping in cosine changes what the dendrogram means.
  • Blocking / BK-trees in record linkage need a metric to guarantee no missed candidate within a radius.
  • Thresholds transfer between datasets only for bounded metrics. "0.85" means nothing for raw Levenshtein.

Try A = (1, 2), B = (4, 6): the 3-across-4-up move gives L1 = 7, L2 = 5, L∞ = 4 — the same displacement, three answers. L1 counts every step you take; L2 measures the shortcut; L∞ only cares about your single worst axis.

Read it as a rule. For any two points, L∞ ≤ L2 ≤ L1, and raising p slides you from "sum of all deviations" toward "just the largest deviation".

Rotation matters. Only the circle (p = 2) looks the same after you rotate the axes. So if your feature axes are arbitrary — PCA components, random projections, embedding dimensions — L1 and L∞ answers depend on a coordinate system that carries no meaning.

04 Euclidean distance L2 · ‖x−y‖₂ · "as the crow flies"

Pythagoras in k dimensions: square every coordinate gap, add them up, take the root. It is the distance your eye already believes in, which is exactly why it is the default — and why people forget it assumes all axes are in the same units.

d(x, y) = √( Σᵢ (xᵢ − yᵢ)² ) = ‖x − y‖₂
Where the square root comes from
Worked example — A = (1, 2), B = (4, 6)
Δ  = (4−1, 6−2)            = (3, 4)
Σ  = 3² + 4²               = 9 + 16 = 25
d  = √25                   = 5.000      ← the classic 3-4-5 triangle
d² = 25                                  (squared version, §05)
✅ Advantages
  • Rotation-invariant — the only Lp that is. Rotate your axes (PCA, random projection) and distances do not change.
  • True metric → safe with KD-trees, ball trees, HNSW, hierarchical clustering.
  • Differentiable and smooth → gradient descent, k-means, RBF/Gaussian kernels all build on it.
  • Units are interpretable: "1.4 units away" means something if your features have units.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Scale-dominated: a salary column in rupees swamps an age column. Standardise or it silently becomes a one-feature distance.
  • Outlier-amplifying: squaring means one wild coordinate can decide the whole answer.
  • Concentrates in high dimensions — all pairs drift toward the same distance (§33).
  • Assumes independent, equally-important axes — correlated features get double-counted (§14).
🐞 Pitfalls
  • Fitting the scaler on train + test together, so distances leak.
  • Using it on TF-IDF: long documents land far from everything simply because their vectors are longer.
  • One-hot columns give every category pair the same √2 gap — a fake uniformity.
  • Comparing distances across datasets after refitting a scaler — the scale changed, the numbers are not comparable.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Features are continuous, standardised, and few-to-moderate in number (< ~30 informative dims).
  • You are running k-means, KNN, DBSCAN or Gaussian-kernel methods on tabular data.
  • Embeddings are already L2-normalised — then it ranks identically to cosine (§11).

05 Squared Euclidean ‖x−y‖₂² · SSE / inertia

Euclidean without the final square root. Because √ is monotonic, every ranking is identical — so if you only need nearest-neighbour order, the root is wasted work.

d²(x, y) = Σᵢ (xᵢ − yᵢ)² argmin d² = argmin d — same neighbours, cheaper
Why it is not a metric — one line on a number line
x = 0, y = 3, z = 6
d²(x,z) = 36        d²(x,y) + d²(y,z) = 9 + 9 = 18
36 ≤ 18 ?  NO  → triangle inequality violated
✅ Advantages
  • Skips n square roots per query — a real win in tight KNN loops.
  • It is the k-means objective (inertia) and Ward-linkage criterion, so the maths stays exact.
  • Expands to ‖x‖² + ‖y‖² − 2x·y, which lets you compute a whole distance matrix as one matrix multiply.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Not a metric → unsafe for triangle-inequality pruning in ball trees / BK-trees.
  • Values are not in data units and are not comparable to thresholds set on plain Euclidean.
  • Outlier sensitivity is doubled relative to L2.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Ranking or optimisation only — KNN scoring, k-means, RBF kernels (which use −γ‖x−y‖²).
  • You need a vectorised pairwise-distance matrix: ‖x‖² + ‖y‖ᵀ² − 2XYᵀ.

Watch the negatives. The matrix-multiply trick can return tiny negative values from floating-point cancellation. np.maximum(d2, 0) before the sqrt, or you get NaNs that surface three steps later.

06 Manhattan distance L1 · taxicab · city-block

You cannot cut through buildings — you walk the grid. Sum the absolute gaps, no squaring, so a single crazy coordinate contributes its own size and no more.

d(x, y) = Σᵢ |xᵢ − yᵢ| = ‖x − y‖₁
3.0
One outlier coordinate, three verdicts — drag the slider and watch L2 run away

L1 grows linearly with the bad coordinate, L2 grows with its square, and L∞ eventually becomes it — the other coordinates stop mattering at all.

Worked example — A = (1, 2), B = (4, 6)
|3| + |4| = 7.000       vs Euclidean 5.000, Chebyshev 4.000
Always: L∞ ≤ L2 ≤ L1        (4 ≤ 5 ≤ 7 ✓)
✅ Advantages
  • Robust to outliers — errors count once, not squared.
  • Cheap: no multiplies, no roots. Great for big brute-force scans and integer features.
  • Its diamond ball has corners on the axes → sparsity. This is precisely why Lasso (L1 penalty) zeroes coefficients while Ridge (L2) only shrinks them.
  • Often ranks better than L2 in high dimensions — it concentrates more slowly (§33).
  • Natural for grid-like reality: warehouse aisles, chip layouts, pixel offsets.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Not rotation-invariant. Rotate the axes and distances change, so it only makes sense when the axes themselves mean something.
  • Non-differentiable at 0 → awkward in gradient-based objectives (sub-gradients needed).
  • Still scale-sensitive; standardising is not optional.
  • Many ties on discrete data — neighbour order becomes arbitrary.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Features are heterogeneous, heavy-tailed, or contain occasional garbage values.
  • High-dimensional sparse counts where L2 has gone flat.
  • Axes are physically meaningful (grid movement, per-field cost of a mismatch).
  • You want a distance that behaves like a median rather than a mean.

07 Chebyshev distance L∞ · maximum · chessboard

Only the worst axis counts. A king on a chessboard reaches any adjacent square — diagonals included — in one move, because the diagonal costs max(1, 1) = 1.

d(x, y) = maxᵢ |xᵢ − yᵢ| = limit of Lp as p → ∞
Worked example — A = (1, 2), B = (4, 6)
max(|3|, |4|) = 4.000
Add a third coordinate that matches exactly → still 4.000 (free dimensions)
✅ Advantages
  • Encodes "any single violation is the whole problem" — perfect for tolerance and SLA checks.
  • Very cheap, and immune to the number of dimensions that happen to agree.
  • Matches machines that move axes in parallel: gantry cranes, CNC heads, plotters.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Throws away almost all the data — one coordinate decides everything.
  • Massive ties: every point on a square shell is equidistant.
  • Not rotation-invariant; extremely sensitive to a single noisy feature.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Quality gates: "flag the record if any field deviates by more than X".
  • Movement cost is parallel rather than sequential.
  • As a cheap lower bound on L2 to prune candidates before exact scoring.

08 Minkowski distance Lp · the family that contains the others

One formula with a dial. p = 1 gives Manhattan, p = 2 gives Euclidean, p → ∞ gives Chebyshev. In scikit-learn this is literally the p argument of KNeighborsClassifier(metric="minkowski", p=…).

d(x, y) = ( Σᵢ |xᵢ − yᵢ|ᵗ )^(1/p) t = p; p ≥ 1 for a metric
Worked example — Δ = (3, 4), sweeping p
p = 1    3 + 4                     = 7.000
p = 2    (9 + 16)^(1/2)            = 5.000
p = 3    (27 + 64)^(1/3) = 91^⅓    = 4.498
p = 8    (6561 + 65536)^(1/8)      = 4.114
p → ∞    max(3, 4)                 = 4.000
p = 0.5  (√3 + √4)²                = 13.928   ← larger than L1: not a metric
✅ Advantages
  • Tunable: treat p as a hyperparameter and let cross-validation pick your geometry.
  • Interpolates smoothly between "count all deviations" and "only the worst one".
  • Same code path for L1/L2/L∞ in every major library.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Another hyperparameter to tune, usually for a small gain.
  • Fractional p (< 1) is not a metric — indexes and clustering guarantees break.
  • Large p is numerically nasty: |Δ|ᵗ overflows fast if you don't factor out the max.
  • Non-integer p is slow — no SIMD shortcut, just pow per coordinate.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • KNN tuning where the shape of the neighbourhood is worth a grid-search dimension.
  • You want to document that L1/L2/L∞ were all considered — this is the family they come from.

09 Cosine distance 1 − cos θ · angular similarity

Forget how long the vectors are; ask only whether they point the same way. That is exactly right for text and embeddings, where length tracks document size or verbosity rather than meaning.

cos(x, y) = (x · y) / (‖x‖₂ ‖y‖₂) similarity, in [−1, 1] d_cos = 1 − cos(x, y) distance, in [0, 2] — NOT a metric d_ang = arccos(cos) / π angular distance, in [0, 1] — IS a metric
1.00 0°
Stretching B moves Euclidean a lot and cosine not at all; rotating does the opposite
Worked example — A = (1, 2), B = (4, 6)
x·y   = 1·4 + 2·6         = 16
‖A‖   = √(1+4)  = √5      = 2.2361
‖B‖   = √(16+36) = √52    = 7.2111
cos   = 16 / (2.2361·7.2111) = 16 / 16.1245 = 0.99228
d_cos = 1 − 0.99228       = 0.00772
θ     = arccos(0.99228)   = 7.125°   → d_ang = 7.125/180 = 0.0396

Now scale: C = (3, 6) = 3·A. Cosine distance A↔C = 0 (identical direction) while Euclidean A↔C = √20 = 4.472. Same pair, opposite verdicts.

The identity worth memorising. For L2-normalised vectors, ‖a − b‖₂² = 2 − 2·cos(a, b). So Euclidean and cosine produce the same ranking once you normalise — which is why FAISS lets you use an inner-product index for cosine search. Check it with the numbers above: √(2 − 2·0.99228) = 0.1243, which is exactly the Euclidean distance between the two normalised vectors (0.4472, 0.8944) and (0.5547, 0.8321).

✅ Advantages
  • Magnitude-invariant — a 40-word and a 4 000-word document about the same topic match.
  • Bounded and comparable, so thresholds (0.80, 0.85) transfer across datasets and are easy to reason about.
  • Behaves far better than L2 in very high dimensions and on sparse vectors.
  • Sparse-friendly: only shared non-zero terms contribute to the dot product.
  • It is what nearly every embedding model was trained with — matching the training objective matters.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Blind to magnitude when magnitude matters — (1,1) and (100,100) are identical; useless for "how much" questions.
  • Not a metric (triangle inequality fails) — use angular distance if an index needs one.
  • Undefined for the zero vector (empty document, all-stopword text) → division by zero / NaN.
  • Ignores an additive baseline shift: two vectors offset by a constant still look aligned. Use correlation distance (§12) if that offset is noise.
  • On non-negative data (TF-IDF, counts) it can never exceed 1, so half the [0, 2] range is unused and similarities look deceptively high.
🐞 Pitfalls
  • Normalising twice — harmless — versus forgetting once, which silently turns your "cosine" index into a dot-product index that prefers long vectors.
  • Reporting cosine similarity where the code returns cosine distance. Half of all threshold bugs.
  • sklearn.metrics.pairwise.cosine_similarity on a 1 M × 1 M matrix — that's a materialised dense matrix and an OOM kill. Batch it or use an ANN index.
  • Comparing cosine scores across different embedding models: 0.8 in one model is not 0.8 in another.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Text: TF-IDF, bag-of-words, sentence/document embeddings.
  • RAG retrieval, semantic dedup, near-duplicate detection, entity resolution on name embeddings.
  • Any high-dimensional sparse vector where length encodes size rather than content.

10 TF-IDF → cosine, worked where the vectors come from in the first place

Cosine needs vectors, and for text those vectors are usually TF-IDF. Term frequency says how often a term appears here; inverse document frequency down-weights terms that appear everywhere. Then you L2-normalise and take cosine — which is why "Inc" contributes almost nothing while "Ardent" carries the match.

tf(t, d) = count of t in d or 1+log(count) — sublinear, tames repetition idf(t) = ln( N / df(t) ) textbook form; sklearn uses ln((1+N)/(1+df))+1 w(t, d) = tf · idf then L2-normalise each document vector sim(q, d) = cos(w_q, w_d) = dot product once both are normalised
The TF-IDF matrix, then cosine of the query against each document

Note cos(d2, d3) = 0.0 — no shared term at all, so the angle is 90°.

For company names, use character n-grams, not words. TfidfVectorizer(analyzer="char_wb", ngram_range=(3,4)) survives both typos and abbreviations ("Ardent Freight" ↔ "Ardent Exp"), which word tokens cannot. The matrix stays sparse, so you can push millions of rows through it and pull top-k neighbours with NearestNeighbors or sparse_dot_topn without ever materialising the n × n similarity matrix.

✅ Advantages
  • Interpretable — you can point at the exact terms that drove a match, which matters for audits.
  • Sparse and cheap: no GPU, no model to serve, trivially incremental.
  • IDF automatically demotes "Inc", "LLC", "Street" without a hand-written stop-list.
  • Strong baseline for entity matching; often beats embeddings on short, noisy, domain-specific strings.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • No semantics — "freight" and "cargo" share nothing. Synonyms need embeddings or a thesaurus.
  • Vocabulary is fixed at fit time: unseen terms in a new document contribute zero.
  • IDF depends on the corpus, so refitting shifts every score — thresholds do not transfer between fits.
  • Word tokens are brittle to typos; character n-grams fix that but inflate the vocabulary.
  • Raw tf over-weights repetition — use sublinear 1+log(tf) for long documents.
🐞 Pitfalls
  • Fitting the vectoriser on train + test together — leakage through the IDF values.
  • Forgetting that TfidfVectorizer already L2-normalises, then normalising again in the metric.
  • Comparing cosine values computed under two different vectorisers.
  • Empty vectors after cleaning (all terms out-of-vocabulary) → zero norm → NaN cosine.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Fuzzy matching company names, addresses, product titles at scale.
  • You need explainability more than semantics.
  • As the lexical half of hybrid retrieval (BM25/TF-IDF + embeddings), which beats either alone.

11 Inner product dot product · MIPS · IndexFlatIP

Cosine's numerator on its own. It is not a distance — bigger means more similar, it can be negative, and it openly prefers long vectors. That bias is sometimes exactly what you want.

score(x, y) = x · y = Σᵢ xᵢ yᵢ ‖x − y‖² = ‖x‖² + ‖y‖² − 2(x·y) how L2, cosine and IP all connect
Ranking equivalence — the rule that saves index headaches
If every vector is L2-normalised (‖x‖ = 1):
  x·y = cos(x,y)                     → IP ranking  == cosine ranking
  ‖x−y‖² = 2 − 2(x·y)                → L2 ranking  == cosine ranking (reversed)
If they are NOT normalised:
  IP favours long vectors            → popular items dominate recommendations
✅ Advantages
  • Fastest possible scoring — one fused multiply-add per dimension, no norms, no roots.
  • Magnitude is a feature in recommenders: vector length can encode popularity or confidence.
  • It is the native score of matrix-factorisation and two-tower retrieval models.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Not a metric, not bounded, not non-negative → thresholds are dataset-specific and meaningless elsewhere.
  • MIPS is genuinely harder than nearest-neighbour search: a long vector can be the top hit for every query, so classic space-partitioning indexes degrade.
  • Silently different from cosine the moment normalisation is skipped — a very common production bug.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Vectors are already normalised and you want cosine at maximum speed (FAISS IndexFlatIP).
  • Recommenders where popularity should legitimately leak into the score.

12 Correlation distance 1 − Pearson r · "cosine after centring"

Mean-centre each vector, then take cosine. What survives is shape: two series that rise and fall together score 0 regardless of their level or amplitude.

r(x, y) = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / √(Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² · Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²) d_corr = 1 − r in [0, 2]; 0 = same shape, 1 = unrelated, 2 = mirror image
Worked example — three series against x = (1, 2, 3)
y = (2, 4, 6)   perfect scaling      r = +1.0   d = 0.0   (Euclidean would say 3.74)
y = (11,12,13)  perfect shift        r = +1.0   d = 0.0   (Euclidean would say 17.3)
y = (3, 2, 1)   exact mirror         r = −1.0   d = 2.0
✅ Advantages
  • Invariant to both scale and offset — the right call for "same pattern, different units".
  • Standard for time-series shape clustering, gene expression, sensor drift comparison.
  • Cancels per-user or per-sensor bias (a rater who marks everything 2 points high).
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Discards level entirely — a fever of 39 °C and 37 °C "match" if they move together.
  • Undefined for a constant vector (zero variance → divide by zero).
  • Meaningless for short vectors: with 2 dimensions r is always ±1.
  • Pearson only sees linear co-movement, and single outliers can dominate it. Spearman if you need rank robustness.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Clustering time series by shape rather than magnitude.
  • Comparing profiles measured on different instruments or scales.
  • Collaborative filtering with per-user rating bias.

13 Canberra & Bray–Curtis relative differences on counts

Both answer the same complaint: with counts, a gap of 5 means something very different at 10 than at 10 000. They divide each gap by the local size instead of treating all gaps equally.

Canberra d = Σᵢ |xᵢ − yᵢ| / (|xᵢ| + |yᵢ|) per-coordinate relative gap; unbounded above by k Bray–Curtis d = Σᵢ |xᵢ − yᵢ| / Σᵢ (xᵢ + yᵢ) one global ratio; in [0,1] for non-negative data
Worked example — x = (10, 20), y = (12, 25)
Canberra    = 2/(10+12) + 5/(20+25) = 0.0909 + 0.1111 = 0.2020
Bray–Curtis = (2 + 5) / (10+12+20+25) = 7 / 67        = 0.1045
Euclidean   = √(4 + 25) = 5.385   ← says "far", ignoring that both counts are large
✅ Advantages
  • Scale-relative by construction — no standardisation step needed.
  • Bray–Curtis is bounded in [0, 1] for non-negative data → thresholds transfer.
  • Canberra is very sensitive near zero, which is a feature when "present vs absent" is the signal (ecology abundance, rare log events).
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Canberra explodes near zero: (0, 1) contributes a full 1.0, so sparse vectors get dominated by noise.
  • Both are undefined/0-by-convention when xᵢ = yᵢ = 0 — libraries differ, check the convention.
  • Neither is meaningful for data with negative values or mixed signs.
  • Bray–Curtis is not a metric (fails the triangle inequality); its square root behaves better.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Species abundance, basket counts, log-event frequencies, read counts.
  • You want relative rather than absolute differences without taking logs.

14 Mahalanobis distance covariance-aware · "how many σ away, accounting for correlation"

Euclidean distance measured in a space that has been whitened by the data's own covariance. Directions the data varies in a lot get discounted; directions it never varies in become expensive. Move along the cloud and you are close; step across it the same physical amount and you are far.

d(x, y) = √( (x − y)ᵗ Σ⁻¹ (x − y) ) Σ = covariance matrix; Σ = I recovers Euclidean d²(x, μ) ~ χ²(k) → an outlier threshold with an actual p-value
0.80
Both probes sit the same Euclidean distance from the centre — Mahalanobis disagrees

P follows the correlation, Q cuts across it. Slide ρ to 0 and the ellipse becomes a circle: both probes converge to the same Mahalanobis distance, and it equals the Euclidean one.

Worked example — Σ = [[4, 3], [3, 4]], centre at origin
det Σ = 16 − 9 = 7
Σ⁻¹   = (1/7)·[[4, −3], [−3, 4]]

Point P = (2, 2)   — along the correlation
  d² = (1/7)·[2,2]·[[4,−3],[−3,4]]·[2,2]ᵗ = (1/7)(4 + 4) = 8/7 = 1.143
  d  = 1.069

Point Q = (2, −2)  — against the correlation
  d² = (1/7)(28 + 28) = 8
  d  = 2.828

Euclidean distance of BOTH points = √8 = 2.828
→ Mahalanobis calls Q 2.6× further than P. Euclidean calls them identical.
✅ Advantages
  • Scale-free — units cancel, so no separate standardisation step.
  • Correlation-aware: catches "impossible combinations" (height 150 cm + weight 120 kg) that Euclidean waves through.
  • d² follows a χ²(k) distribution → a principled outlier cut-off. For k = 2, d² > 5.99 is the 95th percentile, d² > 9.21 the 99th.
  • A true metric, and the foundation of LDA, Hotelling's T², and metric-learning methods.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • You must estimate Σ — it needs roughly n > 5k samples to be stable, and inverting it is O(k³).
  • Singular Σ whenever features are collinear or k > n → no inverse. Needs shrinkage (Ledoit–Wolf), pseudo-inverse, or PCA first.
  • Masking: outliers inflate Σ, which then makes those outliers look normal. Use a robust estimator (MCD) if outlier detection is the goal.
  • Assumes one elliptical, unimodal cloud. On multi-cluster data a global Σ is simply the wrong shape.
  • Harder to explain to stakeholders than "3.2 units apart", and unsupported by most ANN indexes (you must whiten the vectors and use L2 instead).
🐞 Pitfalls
  • Estimating Σ on the full dataset including the test set → leakage.
  • Using the sample covariance on 20 features and 60 rows — the inverse is numerical noise.
  • Forgetting that Σ from training data may not describe production drift; the χ² threshold then misfires.
  • Equivalent shortcut people miss: whiten once with L⁻¹ from a Cholesky factor, then use plain Euclidean everywhere (and get ANN-index support for free).
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Multivariate outlier / anomaly detection on tabular features (fraud scoring, sensor QA).
  • Features are correlated and on wildly different scales.
  • You need a probability-calibrated "how weird is this record" number, not just a rank.

16 Hamming distance count the positions that disagree

Line the two sequences up and count mismatches. It only works on equal-length sequences, because position i must mean the same thing in both — which is precisely why it is unbeatable on bit fingerprints and useless on free text.

d(x, y) = Σᵢ 1[xᵢ ≠ yᵢ] requires len(x) = len(y) normalised = d / len in [0,1], comparable across lengths
Position-by-position comparison
Worked example
x = 1 0 1 1 1 0 1
y = 1 0 0 1 0 0 1
      ↑     ↑            positions 3 and 5 differ
d = 2      normalised = 2/7 = 0.286
Bit trick: popcount(x XOR y) — a couple of CPU instructions for 64 bits at a time.
✅ Advantages
  • Extremely fast — XOR + popcount, hardware-accelerated. Millions of comparisons per second per core.
  • A true metric, so LSH and BK-tree style indexes work directly on it.
  • Exactly right for fixed-width codes: SimHash/MinHash signatures, binary quantised embeddings, DNA reads, error-correcting codes, one-hot categorical rows.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Equal lengths only. One inserted character shifts everything and the distance goes to nearly the full length — it cannot see insertions or deletions at all.
  • All positions weigh the same, and it ignores how different the values are (for numbers, "1 vs 9" ties with "1 vs 2").
  • Coarse: on short codes there are very few possible values, so ties are everywhere.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Comparing hashes/fingerprints for near-duplicate detection (SimHash on documents).
  • Binary-quantised vector search — the reason billion-scale indexes can live in RAM.
  • Fixed-format codes: postcodes, PRO numbers, barcodes of known width.

17 Jaccard, Dice & Overlap set similarity on tokens or q-grams

Throw away order and counts, keep membership. Turn each string into a set of q-grams (or tokens) and ask how much the two sets share. This is what makes word-order differences and long strings tractable.

Jaccard J(A,B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| in [0,1]; 1 − J IS a metric Dice D(A,B) = 2|A ∩ B| / (|A| + |B|) = 2J/(1+J); weights the overlap double Overlap O(A,B) = |A ∩ B| / min(|A|, |B|) 1.0 whenever one set is a subset Tversky T = |A∩B| / (|A∩B| + α|A−B| + β|B−A|) asymmetric generalisation
The two sets, their intersection, and every ratio built from it
Worked example — "night" vs "nacht", bigrams
A = {ni, ig, gh, ht}        |A| = 4
B = {na, ac, ch, ht}        |B| = 4
A ∩ B = {ht}               |A ∩ B| = 1
A ∪ B = {ni,ig,gh,ht,na,ac,ch}   |A ∪ B| = 7

Jaccard = 1/7            = 0.143   → distance 0.857
Dice    = 2·1/(4+4)      = 0.250   check: 2J/(1+J) = 0.286/1.143 = 0.250 ✓
Overlap = 1/min(4,4)     = 0.250

Word-order case: {ardent, express, lines} vs {ardent, express} → J = 2/3 = 0.667, Dice = 4/5 = 0.800, Overlap = 2/2 = 1.000. Overlap says "perfect subset", which is either exactly what you want (abbreviations) or a disaster (every company matching "Inc").

✅ Advantages
  • Order-independent — "Ardent Freight Lines Inc" ≈ "Inc Ardent Freight Lines".
  • Bounded [0,1] → thresholds transfer between datasets and are easy to explain.
  • Jaccard distance is a true metric, and it is the metric MinHash + LSH approximates — so you can do similarity joins over billions of pairs without computing them all.
  • q-grams tolerate typos gracefully: one changed character kills only q grams, not the whole match.
  • Cheap to precompute and invert into a posting list, which makes it a great blocking key.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Blind to order and position — an anagram scores a perfect match on tokens.
  • Sets drop duplicate counts: "aaa" and "a" have identical unigram sets.
  • Size-biased: a 3-token name compared with a 30-token address can never score high, since the union is dominated by the bigger set. Overlap or Tversky fixes this, at the cost of the metric property.
  • Choosing q is a real decision: q=2 is noisy on short strings, q=4 is brittle on typos.
  • Dice is not a metric even though it ranks almost identically to Jaccard.
🐞 Pitfalls
  • Forgetting to normalise case/punctuation first — "Ardent," and "Ardent" share nothing at the token level.
  • Frequent tokens ("Inc", "LLC", "Street") inflating overlap. Weight by IDF, or strip a stop-list.
  • Short strings + q=3: "Li" has no trigrams at all → similarity 0 against everything. Pad the ends.
  • Using Jaccard as the only scorer: it cannot distinguish a genuine match from a token soup that happens to share words.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Record linkage on multi-word fields: company names, addresses, product titles.
  • Near-duplicate document detection at scale (shingles + MinHash).
  • Recommender co-occurrence: "which baskets share items".
  • Blocking: cheap candidate generation before an expensive edit-distance rescore.

18 MinHash & LSH how Jaccard survives a billion pairs

Jaccard on every pair is O(n²) — 100 000 records is 5 billion comparisons. MinHash replaces each set with a short signature such that the probability two signature slots agree equals their Jaccard similarity. LSH then buckets those signatures so that only plausible pairs are ever compared.

P[ minhash_i(A) = minhash_i(B) ] = J(A, B) the whole trick, in one line Ĵ = (matching slots) / k unbiased estimate; error ≈ 1/√k banding: split k rows into b bands of r rows (k = b·r) P[pair becomes a candidate] = 1 − (1 − Jᵗ)ᵈ t = r, d = b threshold ≈ (1/b)^(1/r) where the S-curve turns on
20 5
The LSH S-curve: probability a pair is even looked at, as a function of its true Jaccard

Everything left of the turn-on point is discarded without being scored — that is the speed-up. Everything you discard that was a match is recall you can never get back, which is why b and r are a recall/cost dial, not a detail.

Worked example — k = 100 hashes, b = 20 bands of r = 5
threshold ≈ (1/20)^(1/5) = 0.549

true J = 0.80 → P(candidate) = 1 − (1 − 0.80⁵)²⁰ = 1.000   (always looked at)
true J = 0.60 → P(candidate) = 1 − (1 − 0.60⁵)²⁰ = 0.802
true J = 0.40 → P(candidate) = 1 − (1 − 0.40⁵)²⁰ = 0.186   (mostly skipped)

Fewer, wider bands (b = 10, r = 10) → threshold 0.794, and P(J = 0.6) collapses to 0.059:
much cheaper, and it silently drops genuine 0.6-similarity duplicates.
✅ Advantages
  • Sub-quadratic: candidate generation becomes a hash-bucket lookup instead of a pairwise scan.
  • Signatures are tiny (k integers), so hundreds of millions of sets fit in memory.
  • The recall/precision trade-off is explicit and tunable through b and r — you can compute the curve before you run anything.
  • Backed by a real guarantee, because Jaccard distance is a metric.
  • Streaming-friendly: signatures are built per record, no global pass needed.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Approximate. Some true duplicates never become candidates; that recall loss is invisible unless you measure blocking recall separately.
  • Jaccard only — it does not carry over to cosine (that needs SimHash / random projections) or to edit distance.
  • k, b and r all need tuning, and the S-curve is steep: small changes move the threshold a lot.
  • Useless for very short strings, where q-gram sets are tiny and estimates are noisy.
  • Candidates still need an exact rescore — LSH is stage one, never the final answer.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Deduplicating millions of documents or records (shingles → MinHash → LSH → exact rescore).
  • Blocking for record linkage where a simple key (Soundex, first-4-chars) loses too many pairs.
  • Near-duplicate detection in a crawl or a training corpus.

20 Levenshtein distance edit distance · insert / delete / substitute

The minimum number of single-character edits that turn one string into the other. It is the workhorse of typo correction and OCR cleanup because its cost model matches how the errors are actually made.

D[i][j] = min( D[i−1][j] + 1, delete from x D[i][j−1] + 1, insert into x D[i−1][j−1] + (xᵢ ≠ yⱼ ? 1 : 0) ) substitute / free match similarity = 1 − D / max(len x, len y) the normalisation to prefer
The DP table, filled — and the cheapest path back through it

Every cell is "the cost of aligning the first i characters of x with the first j of y". The bottom-right corner is the answer; the highlighted path is one optimal edit script (there can be several).

Worked example — kitten → sitting
k i t t e n   →   s i t t i n g
1. substitute k → s      (kitten → sitten)     cost 1
2. substitute e → i      (sitten → sittin)     cost 1
3. insert     g at end   (sittin → sitting)    cost 1
                                        total  3

normalised similarity = 1 − 3/max(6,7) = 1 − 3/7 = 0.571
(RapidFuzz's fuzz.ratio reports 61.5 here — a different normalisation, see §23)
✅ Advantages
  • Interpretable — the answer is a number of edits, and you can print the edit script.
  • A true metric (unit costs) → BK-trees and metric indexes work.
  • Handles insertions, deletions and substitutions, so length differences are fine.
  • Costs can be customised: make OCR confusions (0↔O, 1↔l, 5↔S, rn↔m) cheap and everything else expensive, and accuracy on scanned documents jumps.
  • Early-exit and bit-parallel (Myers) implementations make it fast enough for production when combined with blocking.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • O(m·n) per pair — brutal for an all-pairs join. You need blocking, a length filter, or a max-distance cut-off.
  • Unbounded and length-biased: distance 2 is trivial on a 40-character address and catastrophic on a 3-letter code. Always normalise before thresholding.
  • Blind to transpositions: "teh" → "the" costs 2, though it is one slip of the fingers (→ §21).
  • Word order kills it: swapping two words in a name costs almost the whole string (→ §17, §23).
  • Treats every character as equally important — "Street" vs "Straet" and "Ardent" vs "Estas" look similar even though one is a stop-word and one is the entity.
🐞 Pitfalls
  • Not case-folding / stripping punctuation first, so half your distance is formatting.
  • Thresholding on the raw count across fields of different lengths.
  • Unicode: counting UTF-16 code units instead of graphemes, so an emoji or an accented character counts twice.
  • Running it on 100 k × 100 k records "to see" — that is 10¹⁰ DP tables.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • OCR post-correction, spell-check, fuzzy joins on codes and identifiers.
  • Deduplicating near-identical short fields (names, city, PRO numbers).
  • You need to explain a match to an auditor — "one substituted digit" is a defensible reason.

21 Damerau–Levenshtein + transposition · OSA vs unrestricted

Levenshtein plus a fourth operation: swapping two adjacent characters costs 1 instead of 2. Since transposition is the most common human typing error, this is usually the better default for keyboard-entered data.

operations: insert · delete · substitute · transpose adjacent "teh" → "the" Levenshtein = 2 Damerau–Levenshtein = 1

Two different algorithms share the name. OSA (optimal string alignment, what most libraries ship, including rapidfuzz.distance.OSA) forbids editing the same substring twice, so it is not a metric. The unrestricted version allows it and is a metric.
CA → ABC: unrestricted = 2 (transpose CA→AC, insert B). OSA = 3, because it refuses to touch the transposed pair again.

✅ Advantages
  • Matches real typing errors — transpositions are ~10–20% of human typos.
  • Same interpretability and roughly the same cost as Levenshtein.
  • Better than Levenshtein on names entered by hand ("Micheal" / "Michael").
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • OSA variant is not a metric → do not put it in a BK-tree.
  • The unrestricted version needs an extra alphabet-indexed table: more memory, slower.
  • No help for OCR, where errors are substitutions and merges (rn→m), not transpositions.
  • Still inherits every length-bias and word-order weakness of Levenshtein.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Human-typed input: search boxes, CRM entry, manual data capture.
  • Spell-correction candidate generation.
  • Not for machine-generated or OCR'd text — plain Levenshtein with custom costs beats it there.

22 Jaro & Jaro–Winkler built for short names

Not an edit distance at all. Jaro counts characters that appear in both strings within a sliding window, penalises those that appear out of order, and returns a similarity in [0,1]. Winkler then adds a bonus for a shared prefix, because people get the start of a name right far more often than the end.

window = ⌊max(|s|,|t|)/2⌋ − 1 Jaro = (1/3)·( m/|s| + m/|t| + (m − t/2)/m ) m = matches, t = transposed matches Winkler = Jaro + ℓ·p·(1 − Jaro) ℓ = common prefix ≤ 4, p = 0.1
Matched characters, the window that allowed each match, and the transposed pair
Worked example — MARTHA vs MARHTA
window = ⌊6/2⌋ − 1 = 2
all 6 characters match within the window          m = 6
T/H are matched but in swapped order → 2 half-transpositions   t = 1

Jaro    = (1/3)·(6/6 + 6/6 + (6−1)/6)
        = (1/3)·(1 + 1 + 0.8333) = 0.9444

common prefix "MAR" → ℓ = 3
Winkler = 0.9444 + 3·0.1·(1 − 0.9444) = 0.9444 + 0.0167 = 0.9611

More: DWAYNE/DUANE → Jaro 0.822, JW 0.840 · CRATE/TRACE → Jaro 0.733, JW 0.733 (no shared prefix, no bonus) · DIXON/DICKSONX → Jaro 0.767, JW 0.813.

✅ Advantages
  • Best-in-class for short strings — person names, city names, carrier codes.
  • Already normalised to [0,1] and length-aware, so one threshold works across records.
  • The prefix bonus encodes a genuine empirical fact about human error.
  • O(|s|·|t|) worst case but effectively linear for short strings, and no DP table to allocate.
  • The standard in record linkage (it is what the US Census's Fellegi–Sunter implementations use).
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Not a metric — no triangle inequality, so metric indexes and clustering guarantees are out.
  • Scores are compressed high: unrelated short strings routinely score 0.6–0.7, so thresholds live in a narrow, fiddly band around 0.85–0.92.
  • The prefix bonus backfires when the error is at the front — OCR dropping the first character, or "Ltd Ardent" vs "Ardent Ltd".
  • Poor on long strings: the window grows, spurious matches accumulate, and addresses score deceptively well.
  • No edit script — you cannot explain what differs, only how much.
  • Word-order changes are not handled at all.
🐞 Pitfalls
  • Reusing a threshold tuned on first names for company names or addresses.
  • Assuming fuzz.WRatio-style blends are Jaro–Winkler — they are not; check which scorer your library defaults to.
  • Feeding it long concatenated fields ("name + address + city") — the window explodes and everything matches.
  • Case sensitivity: many implementations do not case-fold for you.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Matching person or company names, 3–20 characters.
  • As one feature among several in a scoring model (Jaro–Winkler + token Jaccard + phonetic is a strong trio).
  • You need a bounded score for a rules engine rather than a metric for an index.

26 Combining field scores Fellegi–Sunter · how many metrics become one decision

No real match decision rests on one metric. Each field gets its own comparator — Jaro–Winkler on the name, Levenshtein on the code, Haversine on the coordinates — and each returns "agrees" or "disagrees". Fellegi–Sunter turns those verdicts into log-likelihood weights that add up to a single score.

m(f) = P(field f agrees | the pair IS a match) high for a good comparator u(f) = P(field f agrees | the pair is NOT a match) low for a discriminative field agreement weight = log₂( m / u ) evidence FOR a match disagreement weight = log₂( (1−m) / (1−u) ) evidence AGAINST (negative) total score = Σ over fields then two thresholds: match / review / reject
Click a field to flip it between agree and disagree — the waterfall re-adds

Note how uneven the evidence is. A matching name is worth 9.9 bits because names rarely agree by chance (u = 0.001); a matching city is worth only 4.2 bits because plenty of unrelated records share a city. That asymmetry is the entire value of the method — and it is why "3 out of 4 fields matched" is a bad rule.

Worked example — four fields, three pairs
field    m      u        agree weight        disagree weight
name     0.95   0.001    log₂(950)  = +9.892   log₂(0.05/0.999) = −4.320
street   0.85   0.010    log₂(85)   = +6.409   log₂(0.15/0.99)  = −2.722
city     0.95   0.050    log₂(19)   = +4.248   log₂(0.05/0.95)  = −4.248
zip      0.90   0.020    log₂(45)   = +5.492   log₂(0.10/0.98)  = −3.293

pair 1  all four agree                     total = +26.04  → auto-match
pair 2  street disagrees, rest agree       total = +16.91  → auto-match
pair 3  name and zip disagree              total = +3.04   → review / reject
✅ Advantages
  • Field-aware: rare-value agreements count for more than common ones, automatically.
  • Scores are additive and in bits, so a decision is fully explainable — you can show the audit trail.
  • m and u can be learned unsupervised with EM (this is what Splink does), so you do not need labels to start.
  • Naturally produces a three-way outcome: auto-match, human review, reject.
  • Drops straight into the metrics on this page — each field's "agrees" is just a threshold on its own scorer.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Assumes fields are conditionally independent. City and zip are obviously not, so their combined evidence is double-counted and scores are over-confident.
  • Binary agree/disagree throws away the graded score (fix: level it — exact / close / different).
  • u must be estimated from the data; a bad estimate silently mis-weights everything.
  • Sensitive to missing values — "missing" is not the same as "disagrees".
  • A gradient-boosted classifier over the same field scores usually beats it when you do have labels.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Record linkage / deduplication across systems with several comparable fields.
  • You need an explainable decision (regulated data, audit requirements).
  • You have no labels — start with EM-estimated weights, then upgrade to a supervised model once review decisions accumulate as labels.

27 Choosing the threshold where the metric stops and the business starts

Every metric on this page ends at the same question: above what score do you act? The answer is not a property of the metric. It comes from the overlap of the two score distributions, and — critically — from how badly the classes are imbalanced.

0.85 0.04 100:1
200 true matches against a much larger pile of non-matches — and where you cut

Slide the threshold from 0.75 to 0.90 and watch precision climb while recall falls. Then raise the imbalance: with 100 non-matches per match, a threshold that captured 98% of matches also drags in hundreds of false ones. This is why "our similarity is 0.85+" is not a quality claim.

precision = TP / (TP + FP) of the pairs we merged, how many were right recall = TP / (TP + FN) of the true duplicates, how many did we catch F1 = 2PR / (P + R) only meaningful if the two costs are comparable
🎯 How to actually pick one
  • Label a sample of pairs — a few hundred is enough to see the overlap. Stratify by score so you get examples near the boundary.
  • Plot both distributions. If they barely overlap, the metric is doing its job and the threshold is easy; if they overlap heavily, no threshold will save you — improve the features or the comparator.
  • Choose from the precision–recall curve at the business cost ratio. A wrong merge of two customers is usually far more expensive than a missed duplicate.
  • Keep a review band around the cut and route it to humans. Those decisions become your future labels.
  • Re-validate on a rolling sample: thresholds drift when field lengths, OCR engines or embedding models change.
  • Only ever threshold a bounded, normalised score — never raw Levenshtein or a dot product.
⚠️ Where teams go wrong
  • Picking 0.85 because it appeared in a blog post.
  • Reporting accuracy on a set where 99.9% of pairs are non-matches — "99.8% accurate" is worse than useless.
  • Tuning the threshold on the same pairs used to report the final metrics.
  • One global threshold for every field and every record type.
  • Measuring only the scorer, never the blocker — pairs that blocking dropped are invisible in your precision/recall.

28 Entity resolution pairs → clusters → golden record

Your matcher outputs pairs. The business needs entities — one row per real customer. Turning pairwise scores into clusters is where fuzzy matching most often goes wrong, because similarity is not transitive: A matches B, B matches C, and A and C are different people.

29 KL & Jensen–Shannon divergence comparing two distributions

When your "points" are histograms — a feature's distribution last month versus today — you need a different tool. KL divergence measures the extra surprise of using Q when reality is P. It is not a distance: it is asymmetric, unbounded, and infinite the moment Q assigns zero probability to something P allows. JS divergence is the fix.

KL(P‖Q) = Σᵢ P(i)·log( P(i)/Q(i) ) ≥ 0, asymmetric, ∞ if Q(i)=0 & P(i)>0 M = ½(P + Q) JSD = ½KL(P‖M) + ½KL(Q‖M) symmetric, ≤ ln 2 = 0.693 nats √JSD = a true metric the version to threshold on
2 0.000
Top: the two histograms. Bottom: their CDFs — the shaded gap is Wasserstein-1

Push the shift until the two histograms no longer overlap: KL and JS stop being informative (KL goes infinite without smoothing, JS pins at its ln 2 ceiling) while Wasserstein keeps growing linearly. That is the whole argument for using it on drift dashboards.

Worked example — P = (0.5, 0.5), Q = (0.9, 0.1)
KL(P‖Q) = 0.5·ln(0.5/0.9) + 0.5·ln(0.5/0.1)
        = 0.5·(−0.5878) + 0.5·(1.6094) = 0.5108 nats  (= 0.737 bits)
KL(Q‖P) = 0.9·ln(0.9/0.5) + 0.1·ln(0.1/0.5)
        = 0.9·(0.5878)  + 0.1·(−1.6094) = 0.3681 nats   ← different! not symmetric

M   = (0.7, 0.3)
JSD = ½·0.0872 + ½·0.1163 = 0.1018 nats      √JSD = 0.319  (a metric)
✅ Advantages
  • KL is the information-theoretic answer: it is exactly the extra bits you pay for a wrong model, which is why it shows up in VAEs, distillation and variational inference.
  • Sensitive to changes in the tails, where drift often starts.
  • JS is symmetric and bounded, so it doubles as a comparable score, and √JSD is a genuine metric.
  • Cheap on categorical features — one pass over the counts.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • KL is not a distance: asymmetric, no triangle inequality, and unbounded — "KL = 4.2" means nothing without context.
  • Zero bins blow it up. One unseen category and KL is infinite; you must smooth, and the smoothing constant then changes your numbers.
  • Bin-dependent. Rebin the same data and the divergence changes. Comparing values across different binnings is meaningless.
  • Geometry-blind: moving mass from bin 1 to bin 2 scores the same as moving it to bin 50. Not what you want for ordered/numeric features.
  • JS saturates at ln 2 for disjoint distributions, so it cannot tell "just separated" from "wildly apart".
  • nats vs bits confusion (natural log vs log₂) silently changes every threshold by 1.44×.
🐞 Pitfalls
  • Computing KL on raw counts instead of normalised probabilities.
  • Different bin edges for P and Q — always fix the edges on the reference window.
  • Tiny reference samples: with 200 rows and 20 bins, the divergence is mostly sampling noise. Bootstrap a threshold instead of guessing 0.1.
  • Using KL(new‖ref) in one dashboard and KL(ref‖new) in another and comparing the numbers.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Data / prediction drift monitoring on categorical or binned features (PSI is a symmetrised cousin of this).
  • Comparing a model's output distribution to a reference (calibration, distillation loss).
  • Use JS or √JSD for anything you threshold or alert on; keep raw KL for losses and diagnostics.

30 Wasserstein distance earth mover's · optimal transport

Think of both distributions as piles of dirt. Wasserstein is the minimum work — mass × distance moved — to reshape one pile into the other. Because it uses the geometry of the bin axis, it knows that moving mass one bin over is cheap and moving it ten bins is not.

W₁(P, Q) = ∫ |F_P(x) − F_Q(x)| dx 1-D: the area between the two CDFs = (1/n) Σ |sorted(P)ᵢ − sorted(Q)ᵢ| for two equal-size samples
Disjoint support: the case that breaks KL and JS but not Wasserstein
Worked example — why "disjoint" matters
P = all mass at 0        Q = all mass at 3
KL(P‖Q) = ∞         JS = ln 2 = 0.693 (its ceiling)      W₁ = 3.0

Move Q to 6:
KL = ∞ (unchanged)  JS = 0.693 (unchanged)               W₁ = 6.0  ← still ranking correctly

Samples: P = {1, 2}, Q = {4, 5}
W₁ = (|1−4| + |2−5|)/2 = 3.0
✅ Advantages
  • Geometry-aware — the only option here that knows bin 2 is nearer to bin 3 than to bin 30.
  • Finite and meaningful on disjoint support, where KL is infinite and JS is saturated.
  • A true metric, and it degrades smoothly — perfect for tracking drift over time as a trend line.
  • In the units of the feature itself: "the distribution moved by 4.2 seconds" is a sentence a business owner understands.
  • 1-D case is just a sort: O(n log n), scipy.stats.wasserstein_distance.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Needs a meaningful ground distance on the bin axis. For unordered categories (carrier code, city) there is no natural "distance between bins" and the metric is arbitrary.
  • Expensive beyond 1-D: full optimal transport is O(n³ log n); you fall back to Sinkhorn approximations or sliced Wasserstein.
  • Scale-dependent — comparing W₁ across features requires normalising by spread first.
  • Less sensitive than KL to a change purely in shape that keeps the mass in place.
  • Unbounded above, so thresholds are per-feature.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Drift detection on numeric features — the current best default.
  • Comparing generative model output to real data (WGAN's whole premise).
  • Any time your bins are ordered and the amount of movement matters, not just that something moved.

31 Haversine distance great-circle distance on a sphere

Latitude and longitude are angles, not metres. A degree of latitude is always ~111 km, but a degree of longitude shrinks to nothing at the poles — so Euclidean distance on raw lat/long is simply wrong, and wrong by more the further you are from the equator.

a = sin²(Δφ/2) + cos φ₁ · cos φ₂ · sin²(Δλ/2) d = 2R · arcsin(√a) R ≈ 6371 km; φ = latitude, λ = longitude, in RADIANS
Why one degree of longitude is not one fixed distance
Worked example — Richmond VA → Atlanta GA

Atlanta   φ₂ = 33.7490°  λ₂ = −84.3880°

✅ Advantages
  • Correct on a sphere, cheap (a handful of trig calls), and a true metric.
  • Numerically stable for small distances — that is the reason for the haversine form rather than the spherical law of cosines, which loses precision on nearby points.
  • Supported natively where it matters: BallTree(metric="haversine"), PostGIS, most geo stacks.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Assumes a perfect sphere. Earth is an ellipsoid: expect up to ~0.5% error (≈ 5 km in 1 000 km). Use Vincenty / GeographicLib when survey accuracy matters.
  • It is straight-line distance, not travel distance. For freight ETA, road distance can be 20–40% longer — and that gap is not constant.
  • Slightly ill-conditioned for nearly antipodal points.
  • Cannot be fed to a plain Euclidean index without projecting first.
🐞 Pitfalls
  • Degrees instead of radians — the single most common geo bug; results look plausible but are wrong.
  • (lat, long) vs (long, lat) order: GeoJSON uses (long, lat), most libraries take (lat, long).
  • Averaging coordinates to get a "centre" — the mean of two longitudes across the antimeridian is nonsense.
  • Using kilometres in one place and miles in another inside the same scoring rule.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Any lat/long proximity: nearest terminal, service-area checks, geo blocking for address matching.
  • Validating an extracted address against an expected city — a 600 km gap is a strong "wrong record" signal.
  • For projected local work (a single metro area), a UTM projection + Euclidean is faster and accurate enough.

32 Gower distance mixed numeric + categorical + boolean

Real records are not vectors of floats. They have a weight, a carrier code, a hazmat flag and a date. Gower's answer: compute a distance per column in whatever way suits that column's type, scale each to [0, 1], then take a weighted average.

numeric dᵢ = |xᵢ − yᵢ| / rangeᵢ categorical dᵢ = 0 if equal else 1 boolean dᵢ = 0 if equal else 1 (or Jaccard-style: ignore 0/0 pairs) Gower d = Σᵢ wᵢ·dᵢ / Σᵢ wᵢ bounded in [0, 1]
Worked example — two freight records
column      range        A        B        dᵢ
weight      0–5000       1200     1350     150/5000  = 0.030
carrier     categorical  ARDENT    ARDENT    equal     = 0.000
pallets     0–20         4        6        2/20      = 0.100
hazmat      boolean      true     false    differ    = 1.000

equal weights:  d = (0.030 + 0 + 0.100 + 1.000)/4 = 0.2825

business weights (hazmat is a hard signal, weight 3):
                d = (0.030 + 0 + 0.100 + 3·1.000)/(1+1+1+3) = 3.130/6 = 0.5217
✅ Advantages
  • Handles mixed types without inventing a fake numeric encoding for categories.
  • Bounded [0, 1] and interpretable — you can show a stakeholder the per-column contributions.
  • Per-column weights are a clean place to inject domain knowledge ("a carrier mismatch matters more than a 2% weight difference").
  • Handles missing values naturally: skip that column and renormalise the weights.
⚠️ Disadvantages
  • Range comes from the data, so one outlier squashes every other numeric difference — and a new record outside the training range produces dᵢ > 1.
  • Categorical columns are all-or-nothing: "ARDENT" vs "ARDENT FREIGHT" is as far as "ARDENT" vs "FEDEX" (fix: nest a string metric inside that column).
  • Generally not a metric, and no ANN index supports it → O(n²) pairwise, which caps you at tens of thousands of records.
  • Equal weights are an arbitrary default that quietly encodes a modelling choice.
  • High-cardinality categoricals dominate: with many categories, almost every pair differs → dᵢ = 1 constantly.
🎯 Reach for it when
  • Clustering or deduplicating tabular business records with mixed column types.
  • Entity resolution scoring where each field needs its own comparison rule — this is the standard shape of a record-linkage score.
  • Small-to-medium datasets, or after blocking has cut the candidate pairs down.

This is what production record linkage looks like. Gower is the pattern: per-field comparator (Jaro–Winkler on names, Levenshtein on codes, Haversine on coordinates, exact on flags), each producing [0,1], then a weighted combination — with the weights either hand-tuned or learned (Fellegi–Sunter, or a gradient-boosted classifier over the field scores).

33 Distance concentration the curse of dimensionality, measured

In high dimensions, every point is roughly the same distance from every other point. "Nearest neighbour" stops being meaningful — not because the code is wrong, but because the contrast between nearest and furthest collapses. Below, this is computed live on random uniform points.

Relative contrast (d_max − d_min) / d_mean against dimensionality — higher is healthier

The Lp metrics decay in the order the theory predicts: L1 holds up better than L2, which holds up better than L∞. Cosine decays too but keeps the most contrast here, partly because it is bounded and ignores the shared growth in magnitude that swamps the Lp metrics. One caveat: uniform random points are the worst case. Real embeddings hold up better because they live on a much lower-dimensional manifold than their ambient dimension suggests — which is exactly why you should measure this on your vectors.

⚠️ What goes wrong
  • Ratios of nearest to furthest → 1, so thresholds stop separating matches from non-matches.
  • Hubness: a few points become the nearest neighbour of almost everything, poisoning KNN and retrieval.
  • Index structures degrade to brute-force scans, because there is nothing left to prune.
  • Adding "more features" past a point actively destroys accuracy — noise dimensions dilute the signal.
🎯 What to do about it
  • Reduce dimensions: PCA / SVD / UMAP, or Matryoshka-truncated embeddings (many models are trained so the first 256 dims are usable on their own).
  • Prefer cosine on embeddings, and L1 over L2 on wide sparse tabular data.
  • Select features rather than dumping them all in — what matters is intrinsic dimension, not the column count.
  • Learn the metric: LDA, metric learning, or a fine-tuned embedding beats arguing about Lp.
  • Check the contrast empirically on your own data before trusting any distance threshold.

🐞 How this bites in production
  • A column's unit changes upstream (cents → dollars, grams → kg) and retrieval quality drops with no code change and no error.
  • StandardScaler fitted inside a notebook but not saved into the serving pipeline.
  • Scaler refitted on each batch, so distances are not comparable between batches — and neither are the thresholds.
  • One-hot columns left unscaled next to standardised numerics, so category flips count as ~1.4 σ.
🎯 The fixes
  • Standardise (z-score) for roughly Gaussian features, min–max for bounded ones, robust/quantile scaling when outliers are present.
  • Fit the scaler on train only, persist it with the model, apply identically at serving time.
  • Or sidestep it: Mahalanobis (§14) is scale-free by construction, and Gower (§32) normalises per column.
  • Log-transform heavy-tailed money/count columns before scaling.

import math

def minkowski(x, y, p=2):
    return sum(abs(a - b) ** p for a, b in zip(x, y)) ** (1 / p)

def euclidean(x, y): return minkowski(x, y, 2)          # L2
def manhattan(x, y): return minkowski(x, y, 1)          # L1
def chebyshev(x, y): return max(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(x, y))
def sq_euclidean(x, y): return sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(x, y))

def dot(x, y):  return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(x, y))
def norm(x):    return math.sqrt(dot(x, x))

def cosine_distance(x, y):
    n = norm(x) * norm(y)
    if n == 0:
        raise ValueError("cosine is undefined for a zero vector")
    return 1 - dot(x, y) / n

def angular_distance(x, y):                             # the metric version
    c = max(-1.0, min(1.0, 1 - cosine_distance(x, y)))
    return math.acos(c) / math.pi

def correlation_distance(x, y):
    mx, my = sum(x) / len(x), sum(y) / len(y)
    cx, cy = [a - mx for a in x], [b - my for b in y]
    den = norm(cx) * norm(cy)
    if den == 0:
        raise ValueError("undefined for a constant vector")
    return 1 - dot(cx, cy) / den

def canberra(x, y):
    return sum(abs(a - b) / (abs(a) + abs(b)) for a, b in zip(x, y)
               if abs(a) + abs(b) > 0)

def bray_curtis(x, y):
    den = sum(abs(a + b) for a, b in zip(x, y))
    return sum(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(x, y)) / den if den else 0.0

Mahalanobis — and the whitening shortcut

import numpy as np

def mahalanobis(x, y, cov):
    d = np.asarray(x, float) - np.asarray(y, float)
    return float(np.sqrt(d @ np.linalg.inv(cov) @ d))

# Same answer, but computed once and reusable — and it gives you back
# every Euclidean-only tool (KD-trees, FAISS, k-means) for free.
def whitener(X):
    """Return f(V) -> whitened V, so euclidean(f(a), f(b)) == mahalanobis(a, b)."""
    cov = np.cov(X, rowvar=False)
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(cov)                 # cov = L @ L.T
    Linv = np.linalg.inv(L)
    return lambda V: np.asarray(V, float) @ Linv.T

    return d2 > chi2.ppf(1 - alpha, df=len(x))

def levenshtein(a, b):
    """O(m*n) time, O(min(m,n)) space."""
    if len(a) < len(b):
        a, b = b, a
    prev = list(range(len(b) + 1))
    for i, ca in enumerate(a, 1):
        cur = [i]
        for j, cb in enumerate(b, 1):
            cur.append(min(prev[j] + 1,                    # delete
                           cur[j - 1] + 1,                 # insert
                           prev[j - 1] + (ca != cb)))      # substitute
        prev = cur
    return prev[-1]

def levenshtein_similarity(a, b):
    m = max(len(a), len(b))
    return 1.0 if m == 0 else 1 - levenshtein(a, b) / m

def levenshtein_weighted(a, b, sub_cost=None, default=1.0):
    """Cheap substitutions for known OCR confusions."""
    sub_cost = sub_cost or {}
    def cost(x, y):
        if x == y: return 0.0
        return sub_cost.get(frozenset((x, y)), default)
    prev = [j * 1.0 for j in range(len(b) + 1)]
    for i, ca in enumerate(a, 1):
        cur = [i * 1.0]
        for j, cb in enumerate(b, 1):
            cur.append(min(prev[j] + 1, cur[j - 1] + 1, prev[j - 1] + cost(ca, cb)))
        prev = cur
    return prev[-1]

def osa(a, b):
    """Damerau-Levenshtein, restricted (optimal string alignment)."""
    m, n = len(a), len(b)
    D = [[0] * (n + 1) for _ in range(m + 1)]
    for i in range(m + 1): D[i][0] = i
    for j in range(n + 1): D[0][j] = j
    for i in range(1, m + 1):
        for j in range(1, n + 1):
            c = a[i - 1] != b[j - 1]
            D[i][j] = min(D[i - 1][j] + 1, D[i][j - 1] + 1, D[i - 1][j - 1] + c)
            if i > 1 and j > 1 and a[i - 1] == b[j - 2] and a[i - 2] == b[j - 1]:
                D[i][j] = min(D[i][j], D[i - 2][j - 2] + 1)   # transposition
    return D[m][n]

def lcs_length(a, b):
    prev = [0] * (len(b) + 1)
    for ca in a:
        cur = [0]
        for j, cb in enumerate(b, 1):
            cur.append(prev[j - 1] + 1 if ca == cb else max(prev[j], cur[j - 1]))
        prev = cur
    return prev[-1]

def indel_ratio(a, b):
    """What fuzz.ratio actually returns (0-100)."""
    if not a and not b: return 100.0
    indel = len(a) + len(b) - 2 * lcs_length(a, b)
    return 100 * (1 - indel / (len(a) + len(b)))

def token_sort_ratio(a, b):
    key = lambda s: " ".join(sorted(s.lower().split()))
    return indel_ratio(key(a), key(b))

def token_set_ratio(a, b):
    t1, t2 = set(a.lower().split()), set(b.lower().split())
    inter = " ".join(sorted(t1 & t2))
    s1 = (inter + " " + " ".join(sorted(t1 - t2))).strip()
    s2 = (inter + " " + " ".join(sorted(t2 - t1))).strip()
    if not inter:
        return indel_ratio(s1, s2)
    return max(indel_ratio(inter, s1), indel_ratio(inter, s2), indel_ratio(s1, s2))

Jaro, Jaro–Winkler, Soundex

def jaro(s, t):
    if not s or not t:
        return 0.0
    win = max(0, max(len(s), len(t)) // 2 - 1)
    s_m, t_m = [False] * len(s), [False] * len(t)
    matches = 0
    for i, cs in enumerate(s):                       # 1. matches inside the window
        for j in range(max(0, i - win), min(len(t), i + win + 1)):
            if not t_m[j] and cs == t[j]:
                s_m[i] = t_m[j] = True
                matches += 1
                break
    if matches == 0:
        return 0.0
    k = half = 0                                     # 2. count transpositions
    for i, cs in enumerate(s):
        if not s_m[i]:
            continue
        while not t_m[k]:
            k += 1
        if cs != t[k]:
            half += 1
        k += 1
    trans = half / 2
    return (matches / len(s) + matches / len(t) + (matches - trans) / matches) / 3

def jaro_winkler(s, t, p=0.1, max_prefix=4):
    j = jaro(s, t)
    l = 0
    for a, b in zip(s[:max_prefix], t[:max_prefix]):
        if a != b:
            break
        l += 1
    return j + l * p * (1 - j)

    return (out + "000")[:4]

import math

def kl_divergence(p, q, eps=1e-12):
    """Not symmetric, not bounded. eps smoothing avoids the infinity."""
    p = [x + eps for x in p]; q = [x + eps for x in q]
    sp, sq = sum(p), sum(q)
    p = [x / sp for x in p];  q = [x / sq for x in q]
    return sum(pi * math.log(pi / qi) for pi, qi in zip(p, q) if pi > 0)

def js_divergence(p, q):
    m = [(a + b) / 2 for a, b in zip(p, q)]
    return 0.5 * kl_divergence(p, m) + 0.5 * kl_divergence(q, m)

def js_distance(p, q):                     # this one IS a metric
    return math.sqrt(js_divergence(p, q))

def wasserstein_1d(p, q, bin_width=1.0):
    """1-D W1 on two histograms = area between the CDFs."""
    cp = cq = total = 0.0
    for pi, qi in zip(p, q):
        cp += pi; cq += qi
        total += abs(cp - cq) * bin_width
    return total

def haversine(lat1, lon1, lat2, lon2, R=6371.0):
    p1, p2 = math.radians(lat1), math.radians(lat2)
    dp, dl = p2 - p1, math.radians(lon2 - lon1)
    a = math.sin(dp / 2) ** 2 + math.cos(p1) * math.cos(p2) * math.sin(dl / 2) ** 2
    return 2 * R * math.asin(math.sqrt(a))

def gower(a, b, spec):
    """spec: {column: ("num", range) | ("cat",) | ("bool",), ...} with weights."""
    num = den = 0.0
    for col, meta in spec.items():
        w = meta.get("w", 1.0)
        x, y = a.get(col), b.get(col)
        if x is None or y is None:          # missing -> drop the column
            continue
        kind = meta["kind"]
        if kind == "num":
            d = abs(x - y) / meta["range"] if meta["range"] else 0.0
            d = min(1.0, d)                 # clamp: new data can exceed the range
        elif kind == "str":                 # nest a string metric inside a column
            d = 1 - jaro_winkler(str(x), str(y))
        else:                               # cat / bool
            d = 0.0 if x == y else 1.0
        num += w * d
        den += w
    return num / den if den else 0.0

39 Interview answers

The questions that actually get asked, with answers short enough to say out loud.

When would you use cosine over Euclidean?

When magnitude carries no meaning — text and embeddings. A 40-word and a 4 000-word document on the same topic have very different vector lengths but nearly the same direction, so Euclidean calls them far apart and cosine calls them close. Two extra points worth making: on L2-normalised vectors the two produce the same ranking (‖a−b‖² = 2 − 2cos), so the choice is really about whether you normalise; and cosine holds up better in high dimensions, where Euclidean concentrates. The flip side: if magnitude is the signal — spend, quantity, dosage — cosine throws away your answer.

Is cosine distance a metric?

No. It is symmetric and non-negative, but it violates the triangle inequality. If you need a metric — for a ball tree, a BK-tree, or clustering guarantees — use angular distance, arccos(cos)/π, which is a metric and ranks identically. This matters in practice because ANN indexes prune with the triangle inequality, so feeding them a non-metric can silently cost recall.

Why Mahalanobis instead of standardising and using Euclidean?

Standardising fixes scale; Mahalanobis also fixes correlation. If height and weight are strongly correlated, standardised Euclidean double-counts the shared information, and a point that is unusual because of the combination (tall and very light) looks ordinary. Mahalanobis measures distance in whitened space, so that combination lands far away. Bonus: d² follows χ²(k), which gives you a calibrated outlier threshold rather than a hand-picked cut-off. Costs: you need enough data to estimate Σ stably, Σ must be invertible, and outliers inflate Σ and mask themselves — use a robust estimator like MCD if outlier detection is the goal.

Levenshtein vs Jaro–Winkler for name matching — which and why?

Jaro–Winkler for short strings such as person and company names: it is already normalised to [0,1], it is length-aware, and the prefix bonus encodes the empirical fact that people get the start of a name right. Levenshtein when you need an interpretable answer ("one substituted digit") or custom costs — OCR confusions like 0↔O and 5↔S can be made cheap, which is a big accuracy win on scanned documents. In production I would compute both plus a token-level Jaccard, and let a tuned rule set or small classifier combine them; no single scorer survives all the failure modes.

How do you fuzzy-match millions of records without an O(n²) blow-up?

Two stages. Blocking first: generate candidates with something cheap and index-friendly — shared q-grams (invertible index), a Soundex or first-4-characters key, a length filter, or an ANN lookup on name embeddings. Then score only the surviving pairs with the expensive metric. Measure the two stages separately: blocking is judged on recall (a pair it drops can never be recovered), scoring on precision. Practical additions: rapidfuzz.process.cdist with a score_cutoff, sorted-neighbourhood or canopy blocking, and a human-review band for scores either side of the threshold.

What is the curse of dimensionality in distance terms?

As dimensions grow, the ratio between the furthest and nearest neighbour tends to 1 — distances concentrate, so "nearest" carries almost no information, and you also get hubness, where a few points are the nearest neighbour of nearly everything. Indexes lose their pruning power and degrade to brute force. Fixes: reduce dimensions (PCA, UMAP, truncated embeddings), prefer cosine on embeddings and L1 over L2 on wide sparse data, select features instead of dumping them in, or learn a metric. What matters is the intrinsic dimension, not the column count — which is why 768-dim sentence embeddings still work: they live on a much lower-dimensional manifold.

Which metric for drift detection, and why not KL?

KL is asymmetric, unbounded, and infinite the moment a category appears that was absent in the reference — so alert thresholds are unstable and it needs smoothing that then changes the numbers. For numeric features I use Wasserstein-1: it is geometry-aware, finite even when the distributions no longer overlap, and reported in the feature's own units, so a business owner can read it. For categorical features, √JSD (symmetric, bounded, a metric) or PSI. Whichever you pick: fix the bin edges on the reference window, and bootstrap the alert threshold from the reference sample instead of guessing 0.1.

Jaccard or cosine for comparing documents?

Jaccard on shingles/q-grams for near-duplicate detection — it is a metric, it is bounded, and MinHash + LSH make it work at billion-pair scale without computing all pairs. Cosine on TF-IDF or embeddings for semantic similarity — it accounts for term weights and, with embeddings, for meaning rather than surface form. In one line: Jaccard asks "are these the same text?", cosine asks "are these about the same thing?"

Why does k-means use squared Euclidean rather than Euclidean?

Because that is literally its objective: k-means minimises within-cluster sum of squares, and the mean is the point that minimises squared distance to a set. Use plain Euclidean and the optimal centre becomes the geometric median, which is what k-medians does. Squared Euclidean also skips n square roots per iteration and expands to ‖x‖²+‖y‖²−2x·y for a fast matrix-multiply implementation. The trade-off is that it is not a metric and it doubles the weight of outliers — which is why k-means is outlier-sensitive.

Euclidean on latitude/longitude — what breaks?

A degree of latitude is ~111 km everywhere, but a degree of longitude is 111 km × cos(latitude): about 88 km at 37.5° N and zero at the poles. Treating lat/long as a flat plane therefore inflates east–west distances — 16.6% too high on Richmond→Atlanta. Use Haversine (a metric on the sphere, and numerically stable for near points), or project to a local CRS if you want plain Euclidean inside one metro area. Watch two classic bugs: passing degrees where radians are expected, and (long, lat) versus (lat, long) ordering.

Same query, different nearest neighbour — how do you debug it?

Check the three things that define "nearest", in order. Preprocessing: was the scaler fitted at training time and applied identically at serving? Did a unit change upstream? Are the vectors actually L2-normalised (assert it) — because unnormalised "cosine" is dot product and prefers long vectors. Metric: is the index's metric the one the model was trained with, and is it a true metric if the index prunes? Dimensionality: measure the relative contrast; if it is near zero, the ranking is noise and no metric will save it.

How would you pick a similarity threshold?

Never by intuition. Label a sample of pairs, plot the score distributions for matches and non-matches, and pick the operating point from the precision–recall curve at the cost ratio the business cares about — a false merge of two customers usually costs more than a missed duplicate. Then keep a review band around the threshold for human adjudication, and re-validate on a rolling labelled sample, since thresholds drift when field lengths, OCR engines or embedding models change. And only threshold on a bounded, normalised score: "0.85" is meaningless for raw Levenshtein or a dot product.
