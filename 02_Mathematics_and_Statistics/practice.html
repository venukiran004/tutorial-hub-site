Scenario 1: Statistically Significant but Practically Meaningless

SituationQuestionAnswerpractically meaninglessStepsRecommendation

Situation: You run an A/B test on a checkout button color. With 2 million users, you get p = 0.01 and a conversion rate increase from 4.50% to 4.53%.

Question: The PM wants to ship it. What do you advise?

Answer: The lift is 0.03 percentage points (0.67% relative). While statistically significant (large sample → tiny differences become significant), the effect is practically meaningless.

Steps: 1. Compute Cohen's h: $h = 2\arcsin(\sqrt{0.0453}) - 2\arcsin(\sqrt{0.0450}) \approx 0.0014$ — negligible 2. Estimate business impact: 0.03% × estimated annual revenue. If revenue = $100M, impact = $30K/year 3. Weigh against engineering cost of shipping, maintaining, and testing 4. Check guardrail metrics (latency, engagement, error rates)

Recommendation: Don't ship unless the implementation is trivial. The effect is real but too small to matter. Invest time in higher-impact experiments.

Scenario 2: Multiple Testing Disaster

SituationQuestionAnswerFix

Situation: Your team tested 20 different UI variations simultaneously. Three show p < 0.05. The PM celebrates.

Question: Why should you be cautious?

Answer: With 20 tests at α = 0.05, expected false positives = 20 × 0.05 = 1. The probability of at least one false positive = $1 - 0.95^{20} = 0.64$ (64%).

Fix:

from statsmodels.stats.multitest import multipletests
p_values = [0.01, 0.03, 0.04, 0.08, 0.12, ...]  # all 20
reject, adjusted_p, _, _ = multipletests(p_values, method='fdr_bh')
# Only trust results where reject[i] = True

After Benjamini-Hochberg correction at FDR = 0.05, likely only the p = 0.01 result survives. Report adjusted p-values to the PM.

Scenario 3: Peeking at Results

SituationQuestionAnswerSolutionssequential testingalways-valid confidence sequences

Situation: Your A/B test was designed for 4 weeks, but after 1 week the PM checks and sees p = 0.03. They want to stop early and declare a winner.

Question: What's wrong with this approach?

Answer: Peeking inflates the false positive rate. If you check every day for 28 days at α = 0.05, the actual Type I error can be 20–30%.

Solutions: 1. Use sequential testing (designed for continuous monitoring): O'Brien-Fleming or alpha-spending functions 2. Use always-valid confidence sequences (based on e-values) 3. If already peeked, acknowledge the inflated error and run to completion 4. Use Bayesian framework with posterior probability of superiority

Scenario 4: Simpson's Paradox in A/B Test

SituationQuestionAnswerSimpson's ParadoxFix

Situation: Your global A/B test shows treatment wins (+2% conversion). But when you segment by mobile vs desktop, treatment loses in BOTH segments.

Question: How is this possible? What do you do?

Answer: Simpson's Paradox. The treatment group has more mobile users (who have higher baseline conversion). The treatment is actually worse within each platform.

               Control    Treatment
Mobile users:   30%         70%        (mobile converts at 8%)
Desktop users:  70%         30%        (desktop converts at 3%)

Global average looks higher for treatment because more
of its users are in the high-converting mobile group.

Fix: Check for sample ratio mismatch by segment. Analyze per-segment results. The treatment should NOT be shipped — it hurts both segments.

Scenario 5: Underpowered Test

SituationQuestionAnswerAnalysisRecommendation

Situation: You ran a test for 2 weeks with 5,000 users per group. The observed lift is +3% but p = 0.15.

Question: Your manager says "the test failed — no effect." Do you agree?

Answer: No. Failing to reject $H_0$ ≠ proving no effect. The test may be underpowered.

Analysis: 1. Compute achieved power: With n = 5000 and baseline conversion 5%, power to detect 3% relative lift ≈ 35% — severely underpowered 2. Required sample: ~50,000 per group for 80% power at this effect size 3. Report confidence interval: if CI = [-1%, +7%], the data is consistent with both no effect AND a meaningful +7% effect

Recommendation: Either extend the test, use CUPED for variance reduction, or acknowledge the test was inconclusive (not negative).

Scenario 6: Novelty Effect Confusion

SituationQuestionAnswernovelty effectApproach

Situation: You launched a new recommendation algorithm. Week 1: +15% CTR. Week 2: +8%. Week 3: +3%. Week 4: +1%.

Question: What's happening and how do you get the true effect?

Answer: Classic novelty effect — users click more because it's new, not because it's better.

Approach: 1. Plot effect by cohort (users who saw treatment first time in week 1, 2, 3, 4) 2. Look at "mature users" — those exposed for 3+ weeks 3. Run a holdback: keep 5% of users permanently on old algorithm 4. Measure steady-state effect after novelty wears off (typically 3-4 weeks) 5. The true long-term effect is likely +1-2%, not +15%

Scenario 7: Skewed Revenue Metric

SituationQuestionAnswerApproachesWinsorizeLog-transform

Situation: Your A/B test shows +5% average revenue per user, but the median revenue is unchanged. A few whale users in the treatment group skew the average.

Question: How do you handle this?

Answer: Revenue is typically heavy-tailed. A few extreme values dominate the mean.

Approaches: 1. Winsorize at 99th percentile: cap extreme values, then compare means 2. Log-transform: Compare geometric means (more robust to outliers) 3. Bootstrap: Get CI for the difference in means with proper uncertainty 4. Report multiple metrics: Mean, median, P75, P95 — full picture 5. Delta method: Properly estimate variance of revenue-per-user ratio

import numpy as np
# Winsorize
p99 = np.percentile(revenue, 99)
revenue_winsorized = np.clip(revenue, 0, p99)

Scenario 8: Network Effects in A/B Test

SituationQuestionAnswerSolutionsCluster randomizationGraph cluster randomization

Situation: You're testing a new messaging feature. Treated users send messages to control users, contaminating the control group.

Question: How do you design a clean experiment?

Answer: SUTVA (Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption) is violated — treatment spills over.

Solutions: 1. Cluster randomization: Randomize by geography, team, or social cluster 2. Graph cluster randomization: Use community detection to identify groups with few cross-connections 3. Switchback design: Alternate treatment/control across time periods for the entire population 4. Interference model: Explicitly model the spillover as a function of fraction of treated neighbors

SituationQuestionAnswerFrameworkRecommendation

Situation: Your A/B test shows: engagement +10%, revenue -3%, churn +2%.

Question: Should you ship?

Answer: This is a classic metrics trade-off. More engagement but lower revenue and higher churn — likely engagement is low-quality (e.g., clickbait).

Scenario 10: Sample Ratio Mismatch

SituationQuestionAnswerAction

Situation: Your 50/50 A/B test ended up with 52.3% in treatment, 47.7% in control. Chi-squared test gives p < 0.001.

Question: Can you still trust the results?

Scenario 11: Loss Goes NaN

SituationQuestionAnswerMathematical causesExploding gradientsLog of zero/negative

Situation: Your neural network's loss becomes NaN after 50 epochs of stable training.

Question: What mathematical causes could produce NaN? How do you debug?

Answer: Mathematical causes: 1. Exploding gradients: Gradient magnitudes grow exponentially → weights overflow to inf → inf × 0 = NaN 2. Log of zero/negative: log(softmax(x)) when softmax output underflows to 0 3. Division by zero: Normalization with zero variance, empty batches 4. Large learning rate: Updates overshoot → loss oscillates → diverges

Debug checklist:

# 1. Add anomaly detection
torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True)

# 2. Check for NaN in inputs
assert not torch.isnan(inputs).any(), "NaN in inputs"

# 3. Use numerically stable loss
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, labels)  # internally does log_softmax

# 4. Gradient clipping
torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)

# 5. Lower learning rate, use warmup

Scenario 13: Multicollinearity Problem

SituationQuestionAnswerDiagnosisFixes

Situation: Two features have correlation 0.95. Your linear regression coefficients are unstable (flip sign between train/test splits).

Question: How do you diagnose and fix this?

Answer: Diagnosis: 1. Correlation matrix: $|r| > 0.9$ indicates strong collinearity 2. VIF (Variance Inflation Factor): $VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$. VIF > 10 → severe multicollinearity 3. Condition number of $X^TX$: $\kappa > 1000$ → ill-conditioned

Fixes:

ApproachWhen to use
Drop one featureWhen domain knowledge identifies the redundant one
PCA/factor analysisCreate uncorrelated components
Ridge regression (L2)Stabilize coefficients without dropping features
Elastic NetCombine L1 (selection) + L2 (stabilization)
Domain-driven combinationCreate ratio or difference of correlated features

Scenario 14: Class Imbalance Math

SituationQuestionAnswerBetter metricsPrecision-Recall AUCF1 score

Situation: You have 1% positive class. Your model predicts all negatives and gets 99% accuracy.

Question: What metrics should you use? What mathematical adjustments help?

Answer: Accuracy is meaningless here — majority class baseline = 99%.

Better metrics: - Precision-Recall AUC: Focuses on positive class performance - F1 score: Harmonic mean of precision and recall - Matthews Correlation Coefficient: Balanced measure for imbalanced data

Mathematical fixes: 1. Weighted loss: $\mathcal{L} = -\sum w_c \cdot y_c \log \hat{y}c$, with $w{\text{pos}} = \frac{n_{\text{neg}}}{n_{\text{pos}}}$ 2. Focal loss: $\mathcal{L} = -\alpha(1-\hat{p})^\gamma \log(\hat{p})$ — downweights easy examples 3. SMOTE: Synthetic oversampling in feature space 4. Threshold tuning: Don't use 0.5 — use precision-recall curve to find optimal threshold

Situation: Your model shows a 2% improvement in CTR, but the 95% CI is [-0.5%, +4.5%].

Question: The effect looks real but CI crosses zero. What's your recommendation?

Answer: The CI crossing zero means you can't reject the null at α = 0.05. But:

  1. Point estimate is +2%the best single guess
  2. CI width (5%) suggests the test is underpowered
  3. Bayesian perspective: $P(\text{effect} > 0) \approx 90\%$ (based on CI location)

Options: - Run longer to narrow the CI (need ~4x users to halve CI width) - Apply CUPED for variance reduction - Report: "Directionally positive (+2%) but not yet statistically significant. Estimated 90% probability of positive effect." - If the cost of shipping is low and reversible, consider a phased rollout with monitoring

Scenario 16: Non-Normal Data in Small Sample

SituationQuestionAnswersample meanOptionsLog-transform

Situation: You have n = 25 observations that are clearly right-skewed. You need to compare two groups.

Question: Can you use a t-test? What alternatives exist?

Answer: The t-test assumes normality of the sample mean (via CLT), not the data itself. With n = 25 and skewed data, the CLT approximation may be poor.

Options: 1. Log-transform → t-test on log-transformed data (if data is log-normal) 2. Mann-Whitney U test — non-parametric, tests rank differences 3. Permutation test — exact, no distributional assumptions 4. Bootstrap — resample to get CI for difference in means 5. Trimmed mean t-test — robust to outliers

from scipy.stats import mannwhitneyu
stat, p = mannwhitneyu(group1, group2, alternative='two-sided')

Scenario 17: Bayesian Posterior = Prior

SituationQuestionAnswernot informativeToo strong a priorFix

Situation: You set up a Bayesian model but the posterior looks almost identical to the prior.

Question: What's wrong?

Answer: The data is not informative relative to the prior. Causes:

  1. Too strong a prior: Prior variance is much smaller than likelihood — prior dominates. Fix: Use wider (less informative) priors.
  2. Too little data: Likelihood is too weak to update the prior. Fix: Collect more data.
  3. Likelihood misspecification: Model doesn't capture data-generating process. Fix: Check model fit.
  4. Parameter not identifiable: Multiple parameter values produce the same likelihood. Fix: Reparameterize or add constraints.

Diagnostic: Plot prior predictive vs posterior predictive. If they're similar, the model isn't learning from data.

Scenario 18: Regularization Strength Selection

SituationQuestionAnswerSelection methodsCross-validationGeneralized Cross-Validation (GCV)

Situation: You're training a Ridge regression. How do you choose λ?

Question: Explain the math behind λ selection and its effect.

Answer: Ridge solution: $\hat{\theta} = (X^TX + \lambda I)^{-1} X^T y$

  • $\lambda \to 0$: Approaches OLS (no regularization, may overfit)
  • $\lambda \to \infty$: All coefficients → 0 (underfitting)

Selection methods: 1. Cross-validation: Try $\lambda \in {10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, \dots, 10^{4}}$, pick lowest CV error 2. Generalized Cross-Validation (GCV): Efficient leave-one-out approximation 3. Bayesian view: $\lambda = \sigma^2_\epsilon / \sigma^2_\theta$ (noise variance / prior variance)

from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeCV
model = RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-4, 4, 100), cv=5)
model.fit(X, y)
print(f"Best lambda: {model.alpha_}")

Scenario 19: Feature Importance Disagrees with Correlation

SituationQuestionAnswerFeature A is redundantFeature B interacts with othersLesson

Situation: Feature A has highest correlation with target (r = 0.8) but random forest ranks it 5th in feature importance. Feature B (r = 0.3) is ranked 1st.

Question: Why the discrepancy?

Answer: Correlation measures linear association. Feature importance measures predictive power.

Possible explanations: 1. Feature A is redundant: Other features (C, D) already capture the same information. Once they split first, A adds little. 2. Feature B has nonlinear relationships: Low linear correlation but strong interaction effects or nonlinear patterns. 3. Feature B interacts with others: Its importance comes from splits combined with other features. 4. Feature importance is permutation-based: Measures drop in accuracy when feature is shuffled — captures all types of relationships.

Lesson: Don't use correlation alone for feature selection. Use mutual information, permutation importance, or SHAP values.

Scenario 21: Estimating Conversion Rate with Limited Data

SituationQuestionAnswerFrequentist CIBayesian approachRecommendation

Situation: You launched a new product. After 10 users, 3 converted. PM asks: "What's our conversion rate?"

Question: How do you give a meaningful estimate with only 10 observations?

Answer: Point estimate: 3/10 = 30%, but the CI is huge.

Frequentist CI (Wilson interval, better than Wald for small n): $$\hat{p} = \frac{x + z^2/2}{n + z^2} \pm \frac{z}{n + z^2}\sqrt{\frac{x(n-x)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4}}$$

For n=10, x=3: 95% CI ≈ [10.8%, 60.3%] — very wide.

Bayesian approach (more intuitive): - Prior: Beta(1, 1) (uniform) or Beta(2, 8) if similar products convert ~20% - Posterior: Beta(1+3, 1+7) = Beta(4, 8) - Mean: 4/12 = 33%, 95% credible interval ≈ [12%, 58%] - With informative prior Beta(2,8): Posterior = Beta(5, 15), mean = 25%

Recommendation: Report "Estimated 25-33% with wide uncertainty. Need 100+ users for a reliable estimate."

Scenario 22: Detecting Data Drift

SituationQuestionAnswerStatistical tests for driftKS testChi-squared test

Situation: Your production model's accuracy dropped 5% over 3 months. You suspect the input distribution shifted.

Question: How do you mathematically detect and quantify the drift?

Answer: Statistical tests for drift: 1. KS test (continuous features): Tests if two distributions differ 2. Chi-squared test (categorical features): Tests frequency changes 3. PSI (Population Stability Index): $$PSI = \sum (p_i^{\text{new}} - p_i^{\text{old}}) \ln\frac{p_i^{\text{new}}}{p_i^{\text{old}}}$$ - PSI < 0.1: No drift. 0.1-0.25: Moderate. >0.25: Significant.

  1. MMD (Maximum Mean Discrepancy): Kernel-based test for high-dimensional drift
from scipy.stats import ks_2samp
for col in features:
    stat, p = ks_2samp(train_data[col], prod_data[col])
    if p < 0.01:
        print(f"Drift detected in {col}: KS={stat:.3f}, p={p:.4f}")

Scenario 25: CLT Doesn't Apply

SituationQuestionAnswerfinite varianceEvidenceSolutions

Situation: Your metric is highly right-skewed with extreme outliers (e.g., revenue per user). With n = 1,000, the sampling distribution of the mean still looks skewed.

Question: CLT says the mean should be normal. Why isn't it working?

Answer: CLT requires finite variance. Heavy-tailed distributions (Pareto, Cauchy-like tails) can have: - Infinite or very large variance → CLT converges very slowly - A few extreme values dominate the sum

Evidence: $n = 1,000$ may not be enough for convergence if the tail index is close to 2.

Solutions: 1. Log-transform: If data is lognormal, log-mean is normally distributed 2. Winsorize at 99th percentile: Remove tail influence 3. Trimmed mean: Remove top/bottom 5% 4. Bootstrap: Empirical sampling distribution without CLT assumption 5. Robust statistics: Use median instead of mean

Scenario 27: Sampling Strategy for Rare Events

SituationQuestionAnswerStratified samplingOversampling positivesUndersampling negatives

Situation: You're training a model for a disease that affects 0.01% of the population. Your training data has 10M records but only 1,000 positive cases.

Question: How do you handle this extreme imbalance mathematically?

Answer: 1. Stratified sampling: Ensure train/val/test splits maintain the 0.01% ratio 2. Oversampling positives: SMOTE generates synthetic positives in feature space 3. Undersampling negatives: Random or Tomek links (remove borderline negatives) 4. Cost-sensitive learning: Assign class weights inversely proportional to frequency: $w_+ = \frac{n}{2 \cdot n_+}$ 5. Anomaly detection framing: Treat positives as anomalies, train on negatives only 6. Focal loss: $\mathcal{L} = -\alpha(1-p_t)^\gamma \log(p_t)$ — focuses on hard examples

Evaluation: Use PR-AUC, not ROC-AUC (ROC is overly optimistic for rare events).

Scenario 28: Distribution Shift in Production

SituationQuestionAnswerTypes of shiftCovariate shiftConcept drift

Situation: Your model was trained on data from 2023. Now in 2024, user behavior has changed significantly.

Question: How do you mathematically characterize and handle this shift?

Answer: Types of shift: - Covariate shift: $P_{\text{train}}(X) \neq P_{\text{test}}(X)$ but $P(Y|X)$ unchanged - Concept drift: $P(Y|X)$ changes - Prior shift: $P(Y)$ changes

Detection: KS test, PSI, MMD on features. Monitor prediction distribution shifts.

Handling covariate shift (importance weighting): $$\mathcal{L}{\text{adjusted}} = \sum_i \frac{p{\text{test}}(x_i)}{p_{\text{train}}(x_i)} \ell(f(x_i), y_i)$$

Handling concept drift: Retrain on recent data, use online learning, or sliding window models.

Scenario 29: Correlation Matrix is Not Positive Definite

SituationQuestionAnswerCauseFixesNearest PSD matrix

Situation: You compute a correlation matrix from data with missing values. When you try to use it for portfolio optimization, it fails because the matrix is not positive semi-definite.

Question: Why does this happen and how do you fix it?

Answer: Cause: Pairwise deletion of missing values means each correlation is computed from a different subset of data. The resulting matrix may not be a valid correlation matrix.

Fixes: 1. Nearest PSD matrix: Find the closest PSD matrix in Frobenius norm (Higham's algorithm) 2. Eigenvalue clipping: Set negative eigenvalues to a small positive number (e.g., $10^{-6}$), reconstruct 3. Shrinkage estimator: $\hat{\Sigma} = (1-\alpha)\hat{C} + \alpha I$ (Ledoit-Wolf) 4. Complete case analysis: Use only rows with no missing values 5. EM algorithm: Estimate full correlation matrix handling missing values properly

Scenario 30: Monte Carlo Estimation

SituationQuestionAnswerErrorFor 1% relative errorVariance reduction

Situation: You need to estimate the expected value of a complex function $E[f(X)]$ where $X$ has a known but complex distribution.

Question: How do you use Monte Carlo methods? How many samples do you need?

Answer: $$\hat{E}[f(X)] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N f(x_i), \quad x_i \sim P(X)$$

Error: $\text{SE} = \frac{\sigma_f}{\sqrt{N}}$ where $\sigma_f$ is the standard deviation of $f(X)$.

For 1% relative error: $N \geq \left(\frac{100 \cdot \sigma_f}{\mu_f}\right)^2$

Variance reduction: - Importance sampling: Sample from proposal $q(x)$, weight by $\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ - Stratified sampling: Partition domain, sample from each stratum - Antithetic variates: Use $x_i$ and $1-x_i$ pairs (for monotone functions) - Control variates: Subtract known-expectation function correlated with $f$

Scenario 31: Target Encoding Leakage

SituationQuestionAnswerProblemFix — Regularized target encodingAdditional safeguards

Situation: You use target encoding for a categorical feature with 1,000 categories. Your cross-validation score is amazing but test performance drops significantly.

Question: What mathematical issue caused this? How do you fix it?

Answer: Problem: Target encoding uses the target variable to create features → information leakage. For rare categories, the encoded value is essentially the target itself.

Fix — Regularized target encoding: $$\text{enc}(c) = \frac{n_c \cdot \bar{y}c + m \cdot \bar{y}{\text{global}}}{n_c + m}$$

Where $m$ is a smoothing parameter. For categories with few samples, the encoding shrinks toward the global mean.

Additional safeguards: 1. Leave-one-out encoding in training (exclude current row's target) 2. K-fold target encoding: Compute encoding from other folds 3. Add noise: Gaussian noise proportional to $1/\sqrt{n_c}$

Scenario 32: Feature Scaling Impact

SituationQuestionAnswerFixAlgorithms affected by scalingNot affected

Situation: Your KNN model performs poorly. Features range from [0, 1] to [0, 1000000].

Question: Why is this happening mathematically?

Answer: KNN uses distance: $d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sqrt{\sum (x_i - y_i)^2}$

The feature with range $[0, 10^6]$ dominates the distance calculation. A difference of 1 in this feature overwhelms differences in all other features.

Fix:

MethodFormulaWhen to use
StandardScaler$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$Gaussian-like features
MinMaxScaler$z = \frac{x - \min}{\max - \min}$Bounded features
RobustScaler$z = \frac{x - \text{median}}{IQR}$Outlier-prone features
MaxAbsScaler$z = \frac{x}{\max(|x|)}$Sparse data

Algorithms affected by scaling: KNN, SVM, linear regression, PCA, neural networks. Not affected: Tree-based models (decisions based on thresholds, not distances).

Situation: Each feature individually looks normal, but some data points are anomalous when features are considered jointly.

Question: How do you detect multivariate anomalies that are invisible in univariate analysis?

Answer: Example: A person with income = $50K (normal) and house value = $5M (high but possible) — the combination is anomalous.

Isolation Forest: Randomly partitions feature space; anomalies require fewer splits 3. GMM: Fit mixture model, flag points with low likelihood 4. Autoencoder: Train to reconstruct normal data; high reconstruction error = anomaly

Scenario 34: Dimensionality Reduction Choice

SituationQuestionAnswerFor classification

Situation: You have 5,000 features from text embeddings. You need to reduce dimensionality for a classifier.

Question: PCA, t-SNE, or UMAP? What are the mathematical trade-offs?

Answer:

Scenario 35: Information Gain is Zero

SituationQuestionAnswerPossible causesInteraction effectInsufficient granularity

Situation: Your decision tree won't split on a continuous feature that you know is predictive.

Question: What mathematical issue could cause this?

Answer: Possible causes: 1. Feature is predictive but not through axis-aligned splits: Decision trees split on single features. If the decision boundary is diagonal (e.g., $x_1 + x_2 > 5$), no single split captures it. 2. Interaction effect: Feature is only useful in combination with another feature. Information gain of A alone is zero, but IG(A|B) is high. 3. Insufficient granularity: If the tree has already split on correlated features, the residual information gain of this feature is zero.

Fixes: - Create interaction features ($x_1 \times x_2$, $x_1 / x_2$) - Use oblique decision trees (split on linear combinations) - Use ensemble methods (Random Forest samples different feature subsets)

Scenario 36: Sample Size for Power Analysis

SituationQuestionAnswer~25,000 users per group

Situation: You need to detect a 1% absolute improvement in conversion rate (from 5% to 6%) with 99% power and α = 0.01.

Question: How many users per group do you need?

Answer: $$n = \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \cdot [p_1(1-p_1) + p_2(1-p_2)]}{(p_1 - p_2)^2}$$

With $\alpha = 0.01$ ($z = 2.576$), power = 0.99 ($z_\beta = 2.326$), $p_1 = 0.05$, $p_2 = 0.06$:

$$n = \frac{(2.576 + 2.326)^2 \cdot [0.05(0.95) + 0.06(0.94)]}{(0.01)^2} = \frac{24.06 \times 0.1039}{0.0001} \approx 25,002$$

~25,000 users per group, or 50,000 total. With CUPED (50% variance reduction), you'd need ~25,000 total.

Scenario 37: Log-Transform Decision

SituationQuestionAnswerWhen log-transforming the targetMAPEPitfall

Situation: Your target variable (house prices) is right-skewed. Should you log-transform it?

Question: What are the mathematical implications of predicting $\log(y)$ vs $y$?

Answer: When log-transforming the target: - Model predicts $\hat{z} = \log(y)$, inference: $\hat{y} = e^{\hat{z}}$ - MSE in log space ≈ optimizing MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) - Residuals become more homoscedastic (constant variance) - Predictions are always positive (good for prices)

Pitfall: $E[e^{\hat{z}}] \neq e^{E[\hat{z}]}$ (Jensen's inequality). Need Duan smearing or correction factor $e^{\hat{\sigma}^2/2}$.

Decision framework: - Multiplicative effects → log-transform (prices, revenue) - Additive effects → no transform (temperature, time) - Check residual plots both ways

Scenario 38: Kernel Selection for SVM

SituationQuestionAnswerKernel trickSelection strategy

Situation: Your linear SVM gives 70% accuracy. You know the data is non-linearly separable.

Question: How do you choose the right kernel? What's the math behind kernels?

Answer: Kernel trick: Map data to higher-dimensional space without computing the mapping explicitly. $K(x_i, x_j) = \phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_j)$.

KernelFormulaBest for
Linear$x_i^T x_j$Linearly separable, high-dim (text)
RBF$\exp(-\gamma|x_i - x_j|^2)$Default, most non-linear problems
Polynomial$(x_i^T x_j + c)^d$Feature interactions of known degree
Sigmoid$\tanh(\gamma x_i^T x_j + c)$Neural network-like behavior

Selection strategy: 1. Start with RBF — universal approximator 2. Use grid search over $C$ and $\gamma$: C ∈ {0.1, 1, 10, 100}, γ ∈ {1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 0.1} 3. If features >> samples, try linear first (often sufficient)

Scenario 39: Eigenface Decomposition

SituationQuestionAnswerTrick

Situation: You have 10,000 face images (100×100 pixels = 10,000 features). You want to build a face recognition system.

Question: How does PCA help here? What's the math?

Answer: 1. Flatten each image to a 10,000-dim vector 2. Center: $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$; $\tilde{x}_i = x_i - \bar{x}$ 3. Compute covariance $C = \frac{1}{n}\tilde{X}^T\tilde{X}$ (10,000 × 10,000 — too large!) 4. Trick: Compute $\tilde{X}\tilde{X}^T$ instead (n × n = 10,000 × 10,000). Eigenvectors of this smaller matrix can be projected back. 5. Keep top-$k$ eigenvectors (eigenfaces): $k = 100-300$ typically captures 95%+ variance 6. Project: $z_i = V_k^T \tilde{x}_i$ (10,000-dim → 200-dim) 7. Recognition: Compare projections using cosine similarity or SVM

Scenario 42: Bayesian A/B Test with Prior

SituationQuestionAnswerBayesian approachPrior from previous experimentUpdate with new data

Situation: Your previous experiment showed a 2% conversion lift. Now you're running a new experiment on a similar feature.

Question: How do you incorporate prior knowledge into the new test?

Answer: Bayesian approach: 1. Prior from previous experiment: If prior test showed Beta(200, 9800) for control (2% rate) and Beta(204, 9796) for treatment (2.04% rate), use these as informative priors. 2. Update with new data: Posterior = Beta(prior_α + successes, prior_β + failures) 3. Decision rule: Compute $P(\text{treatment} > \text{control})$ via simulation

import numpy as np
# Posterior samples
control = np.random.beta(prior_a_c + succ_c, prior_b_c + fail_c, 100000)
treatment = np.random.beta(prior_a_t + succ_t, prior_b_t + fail_t, 100000)
prob_better = np.mean(treatment > control)
print(f"P(treatment better) = {prob_better:.3f}")

Scenario 43: Metric Sensitivity Analysis

SituationQuestionAnswerRMSEMAEInterpretation

Situation: Your model improves RMSE by 5% but increases MAE by 2%.

Question: Which metric should you trust? What does this discrepancy tell you?

Answer: - RMSE penalizes large errors more ($\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum e_i^2}$) — sensitive to outliers - MAE treats all errors equally ($\frac{1}{n}\sum |e_i|$) — robust to outliers

Interpretation: The new model is better at predicting extreme values (reduces large errors → lower RMSE) but slightly worse at typical predictions (higher MAE).

Decision: Depends on the business context: - Predicting house prices for buyers → MAE (typical error matters) - Risk assessment where large errors are costly → RMSE - Report both and let stakeholders decide

Scenario 44: Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test False Alarm

SituationQuestionAnswerSolutionsUse PSISet effect size thresholds

Situation: You use KS test to monitor feature distributions daily. It triggers on almost every feature.

Question: Why are there so many false alarms?

Answer: With large production datasets (millions of records), even tiny distribution shifts become statistically significant. The KS test has too much power.

Solutions: 1. Use PSI instead — measures practical magnitude, not just statistical significance 2. Set effect size thresholds: Only alert if KS statistic > 0.05 (not just p < 0.05) 3. Windowed comparison: Compare to recent history, not the training set 4. Alert levels: Warning (KS > 0.05), Critical (KS > 0.15) 5. Focus on prediction drift: Monitor output distribution changes, not individual features

Scenario 46: Multiple Stakeholder Metrics

SituationQuestionAnswerWeighted sumConstrained optimizationPareto frontier

Situation: Marketing wants to maximize clicks, product wants engagement, finance wants revenue. Your model can't optimize all three.

Question: How do you mathematically handle multiple objectives?

Answer: Multi-objective optimization approaches:

  1. Weighted sum: $\mathcal{L} = w_1 \cdot L_{\text{click}} + w_2 \cdot L_{\text{engagement}} + w_3 \cdot L_{\text{revenue}}$ - Pro: Simple. Con: Weights are subjective, can't find concave Pareto front.

  2. Constrained optimization: Maximize revenue subject to engagement ≥ threshold, clicks ≥ threshold

  3. Pareto frontier: Find the set of models where improving one metric necessarily hurts another. Present trade-off curve to stakeholders.

  4. OEC (Overall Evaluation Criterion): Create one composite metric agreed by all stakeholders. Hard but ideal.

Scenario 47: Covariate Shift in Model Retraining

SituationQuestionAnswerCausecurrent data distributionAlso

Situation: You retrain your model monthly. After retraining, performance on historical data looks great but production performance drops.

Question: What mathematical issue causes this?

Answer: Cause: The retrained model is optimized for the current data distribution, which may have shifted from production. If you evaluate on the current training window, it looks good, but production sees data from the evolving distribution.

Also: Potential data leakage in feature engineering during retraining (look-ahead bias).

Fix: 1. Time-based validation split: Always validate on data after the training window 2. Monitor production metrics alongside offline metrics 3. A/B test the retrained model before full rollout 4. Use domain adaptation techniques if shift is expected

Scenario 48: Bootstrapping for Complex Statistics

SituationQuestionAnswerBootstrap variantsPercentile methodBCa (Bias-Corrected Accelerated)

Situation: You need a confidence interval for the ratio of two medians. No analytical formula exists.

Question: How do you use bootstrap to solve this?

Answer:

import numpy as np

data_A = np.array([...])
data_B = np.array([...])
n_bootstrap = 10000
ratios = []

for _ in range(n_bootstrap):
    boot_A = np.random.choice(data_A, size=len(data_A), replace=True)
    boot_B = np.random.choice(data_B, size=len(data_B), replace=True)
    ratios.append(np.median(boot_A) / np.median(boot_B))

ci_lower = np.percentile(ratios, 2.5)
ci_upper = np.percentile(ratios, 97.5)
print(f"Median ratio: {np.median(data_A)/np.median(data_B):.3f}")
print(f"95% CI: [{ci_lower:.3f}, {ci_upper:.3f}]")

Bootstrap variants: - Percentile method: Simple, shown above - BCa (Bias-Corrected Accelerated): More accurate for skewed statistics - Parametric bootstrap: Resample from fitted distribution

Scenario 49: Information Leakage in Time Series

SituationQuestionAnswerCorrect approachesWalk-forward validationExpanding window

Situation: Your time series model achieves 95% accuracy in cross-validation but fails in production.

Question: What went wrong mathematically?

Answer: Standard k-fold CV leaks future information. Folds mix past and future data, so the model "sees" the future during training.

Correct approaches: 1. Walk-forward validation: Train on $[1, t]$, test on $[t+1, t+h]$, slide forward 2. Expanding window: Train on all data up to $t$, test on next period 3. Embargo gap: Leave a gap between train and test to prevent leakage from lagged features 4. Purging: Remove from training any samples whose label period overlaps with test period

Correct:   |---train---|gap|--test--|
            |-----train------|gap|--test--|
            
Wrong:      fold1=[1,3,5,7] fold2=[2,4,6,8]  ← future leaks into training

Scenario 50: Bayesian Optimization for Hyperparameters

SituationQuestionAnswerBayesian optimizationSurrogate model (GP)Acquisition function

Situation: Your model has 10 hyperparameters. Grid search would take weeks. Random search is too random.

Question: How does Bayesian optimization work mathematically?

Answer: Bayesian optimization models the objective function $f(\mathbf{x})$ (validation loss) as a Gaussian Process:

$$f(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}(m(\mathbf{x}), k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'))$$

  1. Surrogate model (GP): Predicts mean and uncertainty of $f$ at any point
  2. Acquisition function: Balances exploration vs exploitation - EI (Expected Improvement): $EI(\mathbf{x}) = E[\max(f^ - f(\mathbf{x}), 0)]$ - UCB:* $\alpha(\mathbf{x}) = \mu(\mathbf{x}) - \kappa \sigma(\mathbf{x})$ (for minimization)
  3. Next point: $\mathbf{x}_{\text{next}} = \arg\max \alpha(\mathbf{x})$
  4. Evaluate $f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{next}})$, update GP, repeat

Advantages: Typically finds good hyperparameters in 20-50 evaluations vs 100s for random search.
