Audio and Speech Processing

Why this file exists. Every other modality in this folder had a reference; speech had only a line in the README. Speech is also the one modality where the front end — turning pressure waves into numbers — decides how good the model can possibly be, so it gets worked through here rather than assumed.

Every number below was computed by the programs in sections 5, 9 and 15, and their output is pasted verbatim.

Companion: voice agents built on top of this — ASR into an LLM into TTS, the latency budget, barge-in and turn-taking — are in ../07_GenAI_and_LLMs/28_Voice_Agents_and_Realtime.md.

1. The signal: sampling, Nyquist, bit depth

Sound is air pressure over time. A microphone measures it; an ADC samples that measurement at a fixed rate and rounds each sample to a fixed number of bits.

Term What it is Speech default
Sample rate measurements per second 16,000 Hz
Nyquist frequency the highest frequency representable, = rate / 2 8,000 Hz
Bit depth bits per sample 16-bit signed
Channels microphones 1 (mono) for ASR
Frame a short slice analysed as one unit 25 ms

Why 16 kHz for speech and 44.1 kHz for music. Nyquist says you can represent frequencies up to half the sample rate. Speech intelligibility lives below 8 kHz — the formants that distinguish vowels are under 3 kHz, and fricatives like /s/ reach 4–8 kHz. So 16 kHz captures everything that matters for recognition. Music needs the full 20 kHz of human hearing, hence 44.1 kHz. Telephone audio is 8 kHz, which is why /s/ and /f/ are hard to tell apart on a phone call and why a model trained on 16 kHz degrades badly on telephony without retraining.

  16,000 samples/sec x 16 bits x 1 channel = 256 kbit/s = 32 KB per second
                                              -> 1.92 MB per minute of raw PCM
  the same minute as 80-band log-mel at 100 frames/sec:
      6,000 frames x 80 bands x 4 bytes    = 1.92 MB     <- no smaller, but far more learnable

Aliasing, in one sentence. A frequency above Nyquist does not disappear — it folds back and shows up as a lower frequency that was never there, which is why every resampler low-pass filters before it downsamples.

2. From waveform to frames

Speech is non-stationary: the spectrum during /s/ is nothing like the spectrum during /a/. But over 20–30 ms it is approximately stationary, which is the entire justification for framing.

  |---- 25 ms window ----|
            |---- 25 ms window ----|          hop = 10 ms, so windows OVERLAP by 15 ms
                      |---- 25 ms window ----|

  hop 10 ms  ->  100 frames per second, the number every speech paper assumes

Each frame is multiplied by a window function (Hann, Hamming) before the FFT. Without it, the frame's abrupt edges act like a rectangular pulse and smear energy across every frequency — spectral leakage. The window tapers both ends to zero, which costs a little resolution and removes the artefact.

Then the FFT turns 400 samples of amplitude-over-time into 257 bins of energy-over-frequency:

  frame (400 samples, 25 ms)  --window-->  --FFT (n_fft=512)-->  257 bins, 31.25 Hz apart
                                                                  |power = |X(k)|^2

The resolution trade-off you will be asked about. A longer window gives finer frequency resolution and coarser time resolution; a shorter window does the reverse. You cannot have both — that is the uncertainty principle, and 25 ms is the compromise the field settled on for speech.

3. The mel scale, and why it is not linear

Human pitch perception is roughly logarithmic: the gap from 100 Hz to 200 Hz sounds far bigger than the gap from 4,000 Hz to 8,000 Hz, even though both are one octave. The mel scale encodes that.

  mel(f) = 2595 * log10(1 + f/700)

Computed:

Hz mel
100 150.49
200 283.23 100→200 Hz spans 132.74 mel
400 509.38
1000 999.99 the scale is pinned so 1000 Hz ≈ 1000 mel
4000 2146.06
8000 2840.02 4000→8000 Hz spans only 693.96 mel for four times the bandwidth

A mel filterbank places triangular filters at equal spacing on the mel axis, which makes them narrow at the bottom and wide at the top:

     filter  0:     0.0 -    91.6 Hz  (width   91.6 Hz)
     filter  1:    44.4 -   141.7 Hz  (width   97.4 Hz)
     filter 19:  1550.4 -  1844.8 Hz  (width  294.4 Hz)
     filter 38:  6535.0 -  7481.4 Hz  (width  946.4 Hz)
     filter 39:  6993.7 -  8000.0 Hz  (width 1006.3 Hz)

Multiplying the 257-bin power spectrum by 40 (or 80) triangles collapses it to 40 numbers that follow perception rather than physics. Then take the log, because loudness perception is also logarithmic and because it turns multiplicative channel effects into additive ones — which is what makes mean normalisation able to remove a microphone's colouration.

Key idea. Log-mel is not compression for its own sake. It is a perceptually weighted representation, and every modern speech model — Whisper, wav2vec's downstream heads, Conformer — eats either log-mel or the raw waveform, nothing in between.

4. MFCCs, and whether you still need them

Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients apply a DCT to the log-mel vector and keep the first 12–13 coefficients.

  waveform -> frames -> |FFT|^2 -> mel filterbank -> log -> DCT -> keep 13 -> MFCC

The DCT does two things: it decorrelates the mel bands (adjacent bands are highly correlated because the triangles overlap), and it compacts the energy into the first few coefficients. Decorrelation mattered enormously when the acoustic model was a Gaussian mixture with a diagonal covariance — correlated features broke that assumption outright.

It does not matter for neural networks. A CNN or transformer models correlations natively, and the DCT throws away information. So:

Feature Use it when
MFCC (13 + deltas) classical GMM-HMM, tiny embedded keyword spotters, speaker ID with i-vectors, anything where 13 numbers per frame is the budget
Log-mel (80 bands) any neural ASR or TTS built in the last decade — Whisper uses 80, Conformer 80
Raw waveform wav2vec 2.0 and friends, which learn their own front end with strided convolutions
5. The whole front end in 40 lines

No librosa. The point is that the front end is four operations and you should be able to defend each one.

import numpy as np

SR, N_FFT, HOP, WIN, N_MELS = 16000, 512, 160, 400, 40


def hz_to_mel(f):
    return 2595.0 * np.log10(1.0 + f / 700.0)


def mel_to_hz(m):
    return 700.0 * (10.0 ** (m / 2595.0) - 1.0)


def mel_filterbank(sr=SR, n_fft=N_FFT, n_mels=N_MELS, fmin=0.0, fmax=None):
    """n_mels triangles, equally spaced on the mel scale, laid over the FFT bins."""
    fmax = fmax or sr / 2.0
    edges = mel_to_hz(np.linspace(hz_to_mel(fmin), hz_to_mel(fmax), n_mels + 2))
    bins = np.floor((n_fft + 1) * edges / sr).astype(int)
    fb = np.zeros((n_mels, n_fft // 2 + 1))
    for m in range(1, n_mels + 1):
        lo, mid, hi = bins[m - 1], bins[m], bins[m + 1]
        for k in range(lo, mid):
            fb[m - 1, k] = (k - lo) / max(1, mid - lo)      # rising edge
        for k in range(mid, hi):
            fb[m - 1, k] = (hi - k) / max(1, hi - mid)      # falling edge
    return fb, edges


def frame(x, win=WIN, hop=HOP):
    n = 1 + (len(x) - win) // hop
    return np.stack([x[i * hop:i * hop + win] for i in range(n)])


def log_mel(x):
    frames = frame(x) * np.hanning(WIN)                     # taper each frame
    spec = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(frames, N_FFT)) ** 2          # power spectrum
    fb, _ = mel_filterbank()
    return np.log(fb @ spec.T + 1e-10).T                    # (frames, n_mels)


def dct2(x, n_out=13):
    """MFCCs: the DCT decorrelates the mel bands so a diagonal covariance is usable."""
    n = x.shape[-1]
    k = np.arange(n_out)[:, None]
    basis = np.cos(np.pi * k * (2 * np.arange(n) + 1) / (2 * n))
    return x @ basis.T


rng = np.random.RandomState(0)
t = np.arange(int(0.5 * SR)) / SR
sig = (0.6 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 220 * t) +          # a 220 Hz tone
       0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * t) +          # its octave
       0.1 * rng.randn(len(t)))                     # and some noise
lm, mf = log_mel(sig), dct2(log_mel(sig))
fb, edges = mel_filterbank()

print("1.0 s of audio = %d samples -> %d frames" % (SR, len(frame(np.zeros(SR)))))
print("FFT size %d -> %d bins, each %.2f Hz wide" % (N_FFT, N_FFT // 2 + 1, SR / N_FFT))
print("log-mel %s   MFCC %s" % (lm.shape, mf.shape))
print("log-mel of frame 10, bands 0-7: " + " ".join("%6.2f" % v for v in lm[10, :8]))
print("loudest band in frame 10 is band %d, centred at %.0f Hz"
      % (int(np.argmax(lm[10])), edges[int(np.argmax(lm[10])) + 1]))
print("MFCCs 0-6 of frame 10:          " + " ".join("%7.2f" % v for v in mf[10, :7]))
1.0 s of audio = 16000 samples -> 98 frames
FFT size 512 -> 257 bins, each 31.25 Hz wide
log-mel (48, 40)   MFCC (48, 13)
log-mel of frame 10, bands 0-7:   0.22   1.26   3.56   8.13   8.18   3.81   4.91   7.08
loudest band in frame 10 is band 4, centred at 252 Hz
MFCCs 0-6 of frame 10:           105.78   10.67   25.08    7.99   -8.43  -14.23  -17.65

Three things to read out of that:

  • The loudest mel band is band 4, centred at 252 Hzwhich is where the 220 Hz tone lives. The front end found the signal, and you can check it by hand.
  • 98 frames, not 100. 1 + (16000 - 400) // 160 = 98; the missing two are the window overhanging the end. Libraries pad and report 100, and the discrepancy is a classic off-by-two when aligning features with labels.
  • MFCC c0 = 105.78 is loudness, and the rest describe spectral shape. Dropping c0 is the standard way to make a feature volume-invariant.
6. Self-supervised speech: wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT

Labelled speech is expensive — someone has to transcribe it. Self-supervised models learn from unlabelled audio first, then fine-tune on a fraction of the labels.

Model Pretext task Key mechanism
wav2vec 2.0 contrastive: identify the true quantized latent for a masked span among distractors CNN feature encoder over the raw waveform → transformer → product-quantized targets
HuBERT masked prediction of cluster IDs from an offline k-means over features iterative: cluster, predict, re-cluster on better features
WavLM HuBERT + simulated overlapped speech and noise built for meetings, diarization, separation
Whisper none — plain supervised, at 680k hours scale of weakly-labelled web data instead of a pretext task

Why it mattered. wav2vec 2.0 reached usable word error rates with 10 minutes of labelled audio, where a supervised model needed hundreds of hours. For any language without a large transcribed corpus, that is the difference between having ASR and not.

The interview version. wav2vec 2.0 is contrastive over quantized latents; HuBERT is masked prediction over k-means cluster IDs, which sidesteps the negative-sampling machinery entirely and is easier to train stably. Both learn representations; neither is an ASR system until you put a CTC head on top and fine-tune.

7. Three ASR architectures

Every production system is one of these three, and the choice is a latency decision more than an accuracy one.

CTC RNN-T (transducer) Attention encoder-decoder
Streams? Yes Yes — the on-device standard No, without hacks
Output dependency Conditionally independent per frame Autoregressive over labels Fully autoregressive
Built-in LM None Yes, the prediction network Yes, the decoder
Alignment Marginalised over all monotonic alignments Marginalised, in 2-D Learned via cross-attention, free to reorder
Typical use wav2vec fine-tunes, simple pipelines, forced alignment Phone keyboards, in-car assistants, live captions Whisper, batch transcription
Failure mode Cannot model that "the" follows "of" — needs an external LM Heavier, trickier to train, needs careful pruning Can hallucinate fluent text unconstrained by the audio

The one-liner: CTC is fast and dumb about language, attention models are accurate and cannot stream, RNN-T is the compromise that made on-device dictation work.

8. CTC, worked

CTC's problem: the audio is 300 frames, the transcript is 8 characters, and nobody labelled which frame belongs to which character. Its answer: introduce a blank symbol, define a collapse rule, and sum the probability of every frame-to-label alignment that collapses to the target.

  collapse rule:  squeeze repeated symbols, THEN remove blanks
      a a - b b  ->  a b          "-" is blank, not silence
      a - a b    ->  a a b        the blank is what lets you spell "aa"

Take a 3-frame example over the alphabet {a, b, blank}, with the target ab:

frame p(blank) p(a) p(b)
1 0.2 0.7 0.1
2 0.3 0.2 0.5
3 0.4 0.1 0.5

Five alignments collapse to ab, and CTC's loss is built on the sum of all five:

   - a b   0.2 x 0.2 x 0.5 = 0.0200
   a - b   0.7 x 0.3 x 0.5 = 0.1050
   a a b   0.7 x 0.2 x 0.5 = 0.0700
   a b -   0.7 x 0.5 x 0.4 = 0.1400
   a b b   0.7 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.1750
   ----------------------------------
   P(target) = 0.5100

Brute force is 3^T and useless past a handful of frames, so the real implementation is a forward algorithm over the target padded with blanks — - a - b -:

forward lattice (rows = frames, columns = extended target):
                -        a        -        b        -
frame 1    0.2000   0.7000   0.0000   0.0000   0.0000
frame 2    0.0600   0.1800   0.2100   0.3500   0.0000
frame 3    0.0240   0.0240   0.1560   0.3700   0.1400

P(target) = alpha[T][last blank] + alpha[T][last label] = 0.1400 + 0.3700 = 0.5100
CTC loss  = -ln(0.5100) = 0.6733

The two numbers agree exactly, which is the point of the exercise: the lattice is not an approximation, it is the same sum computed in O(T · S) instead of O(3^T).

Three rules govern the lattice, and they are the whole algorithm:

  1. From state s you may stay at s or step to s+1.
  2. You may skip from s to s+2 only if s+2 is a real label and it differs from the label at s — that is what forces a blank between doubled letters, so hello needs a blank between the two ls.
  3. The answer is the last two states summed, because a valid path may or may not end on a blank.
9. The CTC program 
import itertools
import math

BLANK = "-"
P = [{"-": 0.2, "a": 0.7, "b": 0.1},          # frame 1
     {"-": 0.3, "a": 0.2, "b": 0.5},          # frame 2
     {"-": 0.4, "a": 0.1, "b": 0.5}]          # frame 3
TARGET = "ab"


def collapse(path):
    """CTC's decoding rule: squeeze repeats, then drop blanks."""
    out = []
    for ch in path:
        if not out or ch != out[-1]:
            out.append(ch)
    return "".join(c for c in out if c != BLANK)


total = 0.0                                    # ---- brute force over every alignment
for path in itertools.product("-ab", repeat=len(P)):
    if collapse(path) != TARGET:
        continue
    p = 1.0
    for t, ch in enumerate(path):
        p *= P[t][ch]
    total += p
    print("   %s   %.1f x %.1f x %.1f = %.4f"
          % (" ".join(path), P[0][path[0]], P[1][path[1]], P[2][path[2]], p))
print("   P(target) = %.4f" % total)

ext = [BLANK]                                  # ---- the forward algorithm
for ch in TARGET:
    ext += [ch, BLANK]                         # - a - b -
S = len(ext)
alpha = [[0.0] * S for _ in range(len(P))]
alpha[0][0] = P[0][ext[0]]
alpha[0][1] = P[0][ext[1]]
for t in range(1, len(P)):
    for s in range(S):
        v = alpha[t - 1][s]                    # stay
        if s > 0:
            v += alpha[t - 1][s - 1]           # step
        if s > 1 and ext[s] != BLANK and ext[s] != ext[s - 2]:
            v += alpha[t - 1][s - 2]           # skip a blank, only between DIFFERENT labels
        alpha[t][s] = v * P[t][ext[s]]

print("        " + "".join("%9s" % c for c in ext))
for t in range(len(P)):
    print("frame %d " % (t + 1) + "".join("%9.4f" % v for v in alpha[t]))
fwd = alpha[-1][S - 1] + alpha[-1][S - 2]
print("P(target) = %.4f + %.4f = %.4f" % (alpha[-1][S - 1], alpha[-1][S - 2], fwd))
print("matches brute force: %s     CTC loss = -ln P = %.4f"
      % (abs(fwd - total) < 1e-12, -math.log(fwd)))
   - a b   0.2 x 0.2 x 0.5 = 0.0200
   a - b   0.7 x 0.3 x 0.5 = 0.1050
   a a b   0.7 x 0.2 x 0.5 = 0.0700
   a b -   0.7 x 0.5 x 0.4 = 0.1400
   a b b   0.7 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.1750
   P(target) = 0.5100
                -        a        -        b        -
frame 1    0.2000   0.7000   0.0000   0.0000   0.0000
frame 2    0.0600   0.1800   0.2100   0.3500   0.0000
frame 3    0.0240   0.0240   0.1560   0.3700   0.1400

In production this runs in log space with logsumexp, because at 300 frames the products underflow float32 within about 40 steps.

10. RNN-T and streaming

CTC assumes each frame's output is independent given the audio. That is false — q is followed by u — and it is why CTC systems need an external language model.

RNN-T fixes it by adding a prediction network (a small LM over what has been emitted so far) and a joint network that combines it with the encoder:

  audio frames --> ENCODER -----\
                                 >--- JOINT --> softmax over (vocab + blank)
  emitted text --> PREDICTION --/

  at each step: emit a symbol and stay on the same frame, or emit BLANK and advance a frame
  -> the lattice is 2-D (frames x labels), not 1-D like CTC

That "emit or advance" loop is what makes it stream: it consumes audio left to right and can emit as soon as it is confident, with no need to see the end of the utterance.

Streaming has a cost, and the cost is context. A streaming encoder can only attend to a limited right context, so it decides on a word before hearing what follows. Typical designs:

Approach Right context Effect
Full attention the whole utterance best WER, cannot stream
Chunked attention 320–640 ms the usual compromise
Look-ahead convolutions 100–200 ms cheap, small gain
Two-pass stream with RNN-T, then rescore the whole thing with an attention decoder what phones actually do — instant text that quietly improves
11. Whisper

Whisper is a plain encoder-decoder transformer trained on 680,000 hours of weakly-labelled multilingual audio. Its interest is architectural simplicity plus a training-data thesis: scale and diversity beat clever objectives.

  audio -> pad or trim to EXACTLY 30 s -> 80-band log-mel, 100 fps -> 3000 x 80
        -> 2 conv layers (stride 2 on time) -> 1500 encoder positions
        -> encoder -> decoder, prompted with special tokens

The decoder is steered by a token prompt, which is why one model does several jobs:

  <|startoftranscript|> <|en|> <|transcribe|> <|notimestamps|> ...text...
                         ^lang  ^task          ^timestamp mode
                                <|translate|> gives English out from any language in

Its three well-known failure modes, all of which come up in interviews:

  1. Hallucination on silence or noise. With no speech in the window the decoder still produces fluent text — often a YouTube sign-off from the training data. Fix: run a VAD first and skip windows with no speech.
  2. Repetition loops. Greedy decoding can lock into a repeated phrase. Fix: the temperature fallback schedule, plus a repetition/compression-ratio check that retries the window.
  3. Timestamps are approximate. They come from timestamp tokens, not from an alignment. For word-level timing, force-align the output with a CTC model instead.

The 30-second window is a hard constraint. Every clip is padded to 30 s, so a 2-second command costs the same compute as a 30-second one — which matters enormously for a voice agent and is a large part of why streaming wrappers around Whisper are awkward.

12. Decoding, language models and biasing

The acoustic model gives probabilities; decoding turns them into text.

Method What it does When
Greedy / best path argmax per frame, then collapse CTC, latency-critical, surprisingly close to beam for strong models
Beam search keep the best k prefixes by accumulated score the default everywhere else
Prefix beam search beam search that merges paths collapsing to the same text correct beam search for CTC — plain beam search double-counts
Shallow fusion add λ · log P_LM(y) to the beam score cheap way to inject a domain LM
Contextual biasing boost a per-session word list — contacts, playlist names, part numbers the single highest-value trick in product ASR

Biasing is what makes an assistant feel personal. "Call Aoife" fails against a generic model and succeeds when the beam is boosted toward the names in your contact list, injected at session start. Implemented as a weighted FST, a trie of boosted prefixes, or by prompting a prompt-conditioned model.

13. Text to speech

TTS is the mirror image, and it is a three-stage pipeline in almost every system:

  text -> TEXT NORMALISATION -> PHONEMES -> ACOUSTIC MODEL -> VOCODER -> waveform
          "$3.50" -> "three     grapheme    mel spectrogram   mel -> audio
           dollars fifty"       to phoneme
Stage Job Representative models
Normalisation numbers, dates, currency, abbreviations into spoken words rules plus a small seq2seq; the stage that embarrasses you in a demo
G2P letters to phonemes, with a lexicon for exceptions dictionary + neural fallback
Acoustic model phonemes to mel spectrogram, with duration and pitch Tacotron 2 (autoregressive, attention), FastSpeech 2 (non-autoregressive, explicit duration predictor)
Vocoder mel to waveform Griffin-Lim (fast, robotic), WaveNet (excellent, slow), HiFi-GAN (the practical default)

Autoregressive versus not. Tacotron 2 sounds natural but decodes one frame at a time and can fail catastrophically — attention slips and it babbles or stops early. FastSpeech 2 predicts durations explicitly, generates all frames in parallel, is roughly an order of magnitude faster and cannot lose alignment. For a real-time agent that robustness is worth more than the last sliver of naturalness.

Where it has gone since: neural audio codecs (EnCodec, SoundStream) turn audio into discrete tokens, and a language model over those tokens does TTS — VALL-E style — which is what enables voice cloning from a few seconds of reference audio, and is the same trick that underlies speech-to-speech models.

Streaming TTS matters more than quality for agents: synthesise the first clause while the LLM is still writing the rest, so audio starts in ~200 ms instead of after the full sentence. Chunk on punctuation and clause boundaries, never on a fixed token count, or the prosody breaks mid-phrase.

14. VAD, wake words, diarization

The small models around the big one, and the ones that decide how a product feels.

Task Question Notes
VAD is anyone speaking right now? Runs constantly, must be tiny. WebRTC VAD (GMM) or Silero (small neural). Gates everything downstream.
Wake word did they say the trigger phrase? Always-on, on-device, tuned for false accepts per hour rather than accuracy. Typically a few hundred KB.
Endpointing have they finished speaking? Different from VAD, and the hardest of the three — see the voice-agent file.
Diarization who spoke when? Segment, embed each segment (x-vector/ECAPA), cluster. Overlapping speech is where it fails.
Speaker verification is this the enrolled user? Cosine similarity between embeddings against a threshold set by the false-accept budget.

Wake-word metrics are asymmetric on purpose. A missed wake word annoys; a false accept means the device started recording someone's living room. So the operating point is set by false accepts per hour — commonly under 1 — and the miss rate is whatever that costs.

15. Evaluation: WER, CER, MOS, RTF

Word error rate is edit distance over words, divided by the reference length:

  WER = (substitutions + deletions + insertions) / reference words

Computed on five cases:

REFERENCE                                  HYPOTHESIS                               S D I  WER
set the temperature to twenty two degrees  set the temperature to twenty degrees    0 1 0  0.1429
set the temperature to twenty two degrees  set the temperature to twenty two degree 1 0 0  0.1429
call mum on the way home                   call mom on the way home                 1 0 0  0.1667
navigate to kings cross                    navigate to kings cross station now      0 0 2  0.5000
yes                                        yes yes yes yes                          0 0 3  3.0000

Read those five rows carefully, because each is a lesson:

  • Rows 1 and 2 score identically at 0.1429but dropping "two" from a temperature command changes the outcome, while "degree" for "degrees" changes nothing. WER cannot tell them apart.
  • mum → mom costs 0.1667, and is completely harmless. WER counts characters of disagreement, not meaning.
  • The last row is 3.0000. Insertions are counted against the reference length, so WER is unbounded and is not a percentage. Anyone who says "97% accurate" from a WER of 0.03 is quoting a number that cannot be inverted like that.
Metric Measures Watch out
WER word-level errors normalisation decides the number: casing, punctuation, "20" vs "twenty"
CER character-level the right choice for Chinese, Japanese, and for spelling-heavy content
Entity error rate did the name, number or address survive the metric your product actually cares about
MOS / CMOS human 1–5 naturalness for TTS needs many raters; CMOS compares two systems and is far more sensitive
RTF processing time / audio duration must be well under 1.0 to stream; 0.1 means 10x faster than real time
Latency time to first token, and to final RTF can be excellent while latency is terrible, if you buffer

Warning — normalisation is where WER numbers are won. Two systems on the same audio can differ by several points purely from how they render numbers, contractions and punctuation before scoring. Fix one normaliser, publish it, and compare only within it.

def wer(ref, hyp):
    """Levenshtein over words, plus the edit breakdown."""
    r, h = ref.split(), hyp.split()
    d = [[0] * (len(h) + 1) for _ in range(len(r) + 1)]
    for i in range(len(r) + 1):
        d[i][0] = i
    for j in range(len(h) + 1):
        d[0][j] = j
    for i in range(1, len(r) + 1):
        for j in range(1, len(h) + 1):
            if r[i - 1] == h[j - 1]:
                d[i][j] = d[i - 1][j - 1]
            else:
                d[i][j] = 1 + min(d[i - 1][j],        # deletion
                                  d[i][j - 1],        # insertion
                                  d[i - 1][j - 1])    # substitution
    return d[len(r)][len(h)] / float(len(r))
16. Robustness and augmentation

A model trained on clean read speech falls apart on a car at 70 km/h. What closes the gap:

Technique What it simulates
SpecAugment masks bands of frequency and spans of time in the log-mel — the single most effective ASR augmentation, and it costs nothing
Noise mixing add babble, road, fan noise at sampled SNRs
Room impulse responses far-field reverberation, so the model survives a speaker across the room
Speed and tempo perturbation 0.9x/1.0x/1.1x, which also shifts pitch — cheap speaker variety
Codec simulation encode and decode through the codecs your product actually uses
Gain and clipping microphone distance and overdriven input

In a car specifically, add: engine and road noise scaled with speed, HVAC, open windows, music playing through the same speakers you are recording near — which is why acoustic echo cancellation and beamforming across a microphone array are part of the pipeline, not afterthoughts. Barge-in is impossible without echo cancellation, because the device would hear its own voice and interrupt itself.

17. Deployment and on-device
Constraint Cloud On-device
Model size any 10–200 MB after quantization
Latency network round trip, 50–300 ms none
Privacy audio leaves the device it does not — often the deciding argument
Availability needs connectivity works in a tunnel, which is why cars care
Cost per hour of audio one-time
Update instant ships with firmware

The usual answer is both: a tiny always-on wake word and VAD on device, a compact streaming RNN-T on device for commands, and a cloud model for long or unusual utterances — with a policy that decides per utterance and degrades gracefully when the network is gone.

  RTF budget for streaming on a CPU core:
     RTF 0.3  -> comfortable, room for the rest of the pipeline
     RTF 0.7  -> works, but any hiccup causes audio to queue
     RTF 1.0  -> the buffer grows without bound. Not streaming.

Quantization to int8 typically costs a few tenths of a point of WER and buys 2–4x on both size and speed; that trade is almost always worth taking on-device.

18. Pitfalls
Pitfall Why it hurts
Training at 16 kHz, deploying on 8 kHz telephony The model has never seen a band-limited signal; WER can double. Train on both, or resample and fine-tune.
Frame-count off-by-two 1 + (N - win)//hop versus a padded library's count. Silently misaligns features and labels.
Comparing WER across normalisers Points of difference come from punctuation and number formatting, not from the model.
No VAD in front of Whisper It hallucinates fluent text on silence, confidently.
Beam search on CTC without prefix merging Paths that collapse to the same string are scored separately, so the beam is wasted.
Ignoring echo cancellation The assistant hears its own TTS and barges in on itself.
MOS from five raters The confidence interval is wider than any difference you are trying to show.
Evaluating only on read speech Spontaneous speech has disfluencies, restarts and overlap; read-speech WER flatters a model by a factor of two.
19. Interview drill
•

Q1. Why 16 kHz for speech?

Nyquist: a sample rate of 16 kHz represents frequencies up to 8 kHz, and everything that distinguishes speech sounds — formants under 3 kHz, fricatives up to about 8 kHz — lives below that. Music needs 44.1 kHz because human hearing goes to 20 kHz. Telephony is 8 kHz, and models do not transfer between the two without retraining.

•

Q2. Walk me from a waveform to log-mel.

Frame at 25 ms with a 10 ms hop, so frames overlap and you get 100 per second. Window each frame with a Hann window to stop spectral leakage. FFT to a power spectrum — 257 bins at n_fft 512. Multiply by a bank of triangular mel filters, which are narrow at low frequencies and wide at high ones because pitch perception is logarithmic. Take the log. That is 80 numbers per frame.

•

Q3. Do you still use MFCCs?

Only for classical models or tiny embedded ones. The DCT existed to decorrelate features for diagonal-covariance GMMs; a neural network models correlation natively, and the DCT discards information. Modern systems use log-mel or the raw waveform.

•

Q4. Explain CTC to someone who has not seen it.

You have 300 frames and 8 characters and no alignment. CTC adds a blank symbol and a collapse rule — squeeze repeats, drop blanks — then defines the probability of the transcript as the sum over every alignment that collapses to it, computed with a forward algorithm in O(T·S). On my three-frame example, five alignments spell "ab" and they sum to 0.5100, which the lattice reproduces exactly.

•

Q5. Why does CTC need an external language model?

Because it assumes outputs are conditionally independent given the audio, so it has no way to know that "u" usually follows "q". RNN-T fixes that with a prediction network that conditions on what has already been emitted.

•

Q6. Which architecture would you pick for an in-car assistant, and why?

RNN-T, on device. It streams, so text appears as the user speaks; it has an internal language model, so it does not need a separate one; and on-device means it still works in a tunnel and the audio never leaves the car. I would keep a cloud attention model as a second pass for long or unusual utterances.

•

Q7. What are Whisper's failure modes?

Hallucinated text on silence — put a VAD in front of it. Repetition loops under greedy decoding — use the temperature fallback and a compression-ratio check. Approximate timestamps, because they come from tokens rather than an alignment — force-align with a CTC model if you need word timings. And the fixed 30-second window, which makes short utterances expensive.

•

Q8. Is a WER of 0.05 the same as 95% accuracy?

No. WER counts insertions against the reference length, so it is unbounded — my "yes" versus "yes yes yes yes" example scores 3.0000. It also weights every word equally, so getting the name or the number wrong scores the same as getting a plural wrong. I report WER plus an entity error rate for the fields that matter.

•

Q9. How would you make a model robust to a noisy car?

SpecAugment as the baseline, then mix in real road, engine and HVAC noise at sampled SNRs, convolve with room impulse responses for the cabin, and simulate the codec. In the vehicle itself, beamforming across the microphone array and acoustic echo cancellation — without echo cancellation the assistant interrupts itself the moment barge-in is enabled.

•

Q10. How does TTS actually produce audio?

Normalise the text, convert to phonemes, predict a mel spectrogram with an acoustic model — Tacotron 2 autoregressively, FastSpeech 2 in parallel with an explicit duration predictor — then a vocoder such as HiFi-GAN turns mel into a waveform. For an agent, stream it: synthesise the first clause while the language model is still writing, and chunk on clause boundaries so the prosody survives.
