AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting)

Combine many weak learners into a strong one by making each new learner focus on the mistakes of the previous ones.

•

1. Definition

sequentiallyreweightedweighted vote

AdaBoost builds an ensemble sequentially: each new weak learner is trained on a reweighted dataset emphasizing the examples previous learners got wrong. The final prediction is a weighted vote, where more accurate learners get more say.

•

2. Intuition & how it works

higher weightThe loopIncreasedecrease

Like a study group that spends more time on missed questions: misclassified points get higher weight so the next stump focuses on them; easy points fade.

The loop: 1. Equal weights on all points. 2. Train a weak learner; compute its weighted error $\varepsilon$. 3. Give it a vote $\alpha$ based on how good it is. 4. Increase weights on misclassified, decrease on correct. 5. Repeat; final model = weighted sum of learners.

•

3. Mathematical formulation (with the update)

Learner weightSample reweightingWorked snippetFinal classifier

Learner weight for weighted error $\varepsilon_t$:

$$\alpha_t=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}$$

  • $\varepsilon_t=0.5$ (random) → $\alpha_t=0$; $\varepsilon_t\to0$ → large $\alpha_t$; $\varepsilon_t>0.5$ → negative $\alpha$ (flip predictions).

Sample reweighting ($y_i,\hat y_i\in{-1,+1}$):

$$w_i\leftarrow w_i\,e^{-\alpha_t y_i\hat y_i}, \quad\text{then normalize}$$

Correct → weight shrinks; wrong → weight grows.

Worked snippet. With $\varepsilon_t=0.3$: $\alpha_t=\tfrac12\ln(0.7/0.3)=0.42$. A misclassified point's weight ×$e^{0.42}=1.52$ (up); a correct point's ×$e^{-0.42}=0.65$ (down). After normalizing, hard points carry more mass into round $t{+}1$.

Final classifier: $\hat y=\operatorname{sign}\big(\sum_t\alpha_t h_t(\mathbf{x})\big)$. AdaBoost is provably forward stagewise additive modeling under exponential loss $L=\sum_i e^{-y_i f(\mathbf{x}_i)}$.

12. Interview deep dive

Q1. AdaBoost vs bagging/RF?Exponential lossQ3. Why sensitive to noise?Q4. AdaBoost vs Gradient Boosting?Q5. Good weak learner?Q6. What is $\alphat$?

Q1. AdaBoost vs bagging/RF? Bagging trains independent trees in parallel and averages (↓variance); AdaBoost trains sequentially, reweighting errors (↓bias), and weights learners by accuracy.

Q2. What loss does AdaBoost minimize? Exponential loss, via forward stagewise additive modeling.

Q3. Why sensitive to noise? Exponential loss grows fast for misclassified points, so mislabels accumulate enormous weight.

Q4. AdaBoost vs Gradient Boosting? Both sequential boosting; AdaBoost reweights samples (special case of exponential-loss boosting), GBM fits the gradient of an arbitrary loss — more general and robust.

Q5. Good weak learner? Slightly better than random (decision stumps).

Q6. What is $\alpha_t$? The learner's vote weight from its error: $\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}$.

Q7. What if $\varepsilon>0.5$? $\alpha<0$ → the learner's predictions are flipped (or boosting stops).

Q8. Role of learning rate? Shrinks each learner's contribution; lower rate + more rounds generalizes better.

•

13. Common mistakes & best practices

  • ❌ AdaBoost on noisy/mislabeled data (overfits the noise).
  • ❌ A too-strong base learner.
  • ❌ Cranking n_estimators without lowering learning_rate.
  • ✅ Keep base learners weak (stumps); tune n_estimators × learning_rate jointly.
  • ✅ Clean labels / remove gross outliers first.
  • ✅ Prefer Gradient Boosting / XGBoost when robustness or top accuracy matters.

14. One-paragraph summary

exponential lossRelated

AdaBoost sequentially fits weak learners (stumps), reweighting misclassified points up ($w_i\leftarrow w_ie^{-\alpha_t y_i\hat y_i}$) and weighting each learner by its accuracy ($\alpha_t=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}$); the final vote is $\operatorname{sign}(\sum_t\alpha_t h_t)$. It is provably forward stagewise additive modeling under exponential loss, turns weak learners strong with little tuning, but its exponential loss makes it very sensitive to noisy labels and outliers — which is why gradient boosting (gradient of an arbitrary, more robust loss) usually wins.

ADASYN (Adaptive Synthetic Sampling)

Fix class imbalance by synthesizing minority-class examples — and make more of them where the minority is hardest to learn (near the decision boundary).

1. Definition

oversamplingmore

ADASYN is an oversampling method for imbalanced classification. It creates synthetic minority-class samples by interpolating between existing minority points, but — unlike SMOTE — it generates more synthetic samples for minority points that are harder to learn (those surrounded by majority-class neighbors), adaptively shifting the decision boundary toward the difficult region.

•

2. Intuition & how it works

SMOTEADASYN

Imbalance (say 99% negative / 1% positive) makes classifiers ignore the minority. Random oversampling (duplicating minority rows) overfits; SMOTE synthesizes new points evenly along lines between minority neighbors. ADASYN goes further: it asks "which minority points are hardest?" — those with many majority neighbors, i.e. near the boundary or in majority territory — and concentrates new synthetic samples there.

SMOTE: equal synthesis per minority point
ADASYN: more synthesis where minority is outnumbered (boundary), fewer in safe interior

                       ●●●●  ← interior minority gets FEW
•

3. Mathematical formulation

Total to generateDifficulty ratiomajorityNormalizeAllocateGenerate
  1. Total to generate. With $m_{maj},m_{min}$ counts and balance level $\beta\in(0,1]$: $G=(m_{maj}-m_{min})\cdot\beta$ ($\beta=1$ → fully balanced).
  2. Difficulty ratio per minority point $x_i$: among its $k$ nearest neighbors, let $\Delta_i$ be the number that are majority class; $r_i=\Delta_i/k$. (Boundary points → high $r_i$.)
  3. Normalize into a distribution: $\hat r_i=r_i/\sum_j r_j$.
  4. Allocate synthetic samples: $g_i=\hat r_i\cdot G$ (more for high-$r_i$ points).
  5. Generate $g_i$ samples for $x_i$: pick a random minority neighbor $x_{zi}$ and interpolate $x_{new}=x_i+\lambda(x_{zi}-x_i),\ \lambda\sim U(0,1)$.

The $\hat r_i$ weighting is the whole difference from SMOTE: it puts synthetic mass where the minority is outnumbered, so the classifier sees more "hard" positive examples.

Worked sketch. Minority point A has 4/5 majority neighbors ($r_A=0.8$); point B has 1/5 ($r_B=0.2$). Then $\hat r_A=0.8,\ \hat r_B=0.2$, so A receives 4× as many synthetic samples as B — densifying the boundary, not the safe interior.

•

12. Interview deep dive

Q1. ADASYN vs SMOTE?moremajorityamplify noise/outliersQ5. Oversampling vs classweight?not

Q1. ADASYN vs SMOTE? Both synthesize via interpolation, but ADASYN generates more samples for minority points with many majority neighbors (hard/boundary cases), while SMOTE treats all minority points equally.

Q2. How does ADASYN measure difficulty? $r_i$ = fraction of a minority point's k-NN that belong to the majority class; normalized into a sampling distribution.

Q3. What's ADASYN's main weakness vs SMOTE? It deliberately densifies the boundary, so it can amplify noise/outliers there.

Q4. Why must you resample only the training fold? Synthesizing before the split leaks information into the test set → over-optimistic metrics. Use imblearn.Pipeline inside CV.

Q5. Oversampling vs class_weight? Resampling changes the data; class_weight reweights the loss. Often class_weight is the cheaper first try; combine or compare.

Q6. Does ADASYN help tree ensembles? Sometimes — but GBMs/RF with class_weight / scale_pos_weight often handle imbalance well already; benchmark both.

Q7. How do you evaluate with imbalance? PR-AUC, recall/F1 on the minority, balanced accuracy — not raw accuracy.

•

moreamplify noise/outliersmustRelated

ADASYN is an adaptive oversampling technique for class imbalance: it computes how "hard" each minority point is (the fraction $r_i$ of its k-NN that are majority), then synthesizes more new minority points — by interpolation $x_{new}=x_i+\lambda(x_{zi}-x_i)$ — around the hard, boundary-adjacent cases and fewer in the safe interior, nudging the decision boundary toward the difficult region. ARIMA (Time-Series Forecasting)

Forecast a time series from its own past: AR(autoregression) + I(differencing for stationarity) + MA(moving average of past errors).

1. Definition

past valuesforecast errorsdifferencing

ARIMA models a stationary time series as a linear combination of its own past values (AR), past forecast errors (MA), after differencing the series $d$ times to remove trend (I = integrated). Orders $(p,d,q)$ specify how many of each.

•

2. Intuition & how it works

AR(p) — autoregressionI(d) — integrateddifferencesMA(q) — moving averageshocks/errorsstationary

Three ingredients:

  • AR(p) — autoregression: today depends on the last $p$ values ("momentum"). e.g. today's sales ≈ a blend of the last few days.
  • I(d) — integrated: if the series trends (non-stationary), take differences ($X_t-X_{t-1}$) until it's flat/stationary. $d$ = how many times.
  • MA(q) — moving average: today depends on the last $q$ shocks/errors (corrects for recent surprises).

Fit on a stationary series (constant mean/variance), forecast, then "un-difference" to get predictions on the original scale.

•

3. Mathematical formulation

AR(p)MA(q)Differencing (I)ARIMA(p,d,q)Choosing ordersStationarity

AR(p): $X_t=c+\phi_1 X_{t-1}+\dots+\phi_p X_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t$

MA(q): $X_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\dots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}$

Differencing (I): $\nabla X_t=X_t-X_{t-1}$; apply $d$ times. Using the lag operator $L$ ($LX_t=X_{t-1}$), the full ARIMA(p,d,q):

$$\Big(1-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i L^i\Big)(1-L)^d X_t=\Big(1+\sum_{i=1}^q\theta_i L^i\Big)\varepsilon_t$$

Choosing orders: - Stationarity: check with the ADF test; difference until stationary → sets $d$. - PACF cuts off after lag $p$ → AR order; ACF cuts off after lag $q$ → MA order. - Compare candidates by AIC/BIC; auto_arima automates the search.

•

12. Interview deep dive

Q1. What do p, d, q mean?Q3. How do you choose p and q?Q4. How do you test stationarity?ADFQ5. ARIMA vs SARIMA?seasonal

Q1. What do p, d, q mean? AR lags, number of differences for stationarity, MA (error) lags.

Q2. What is stationarity and why required? Constant mean/variance/autocorrelation; ARIMA's math assumes it — achieved via differencing.

Q3. How do you choose p and q? PACF cutoff → $p$ (AR); ACF cutoff → $q$ (MA); confirm with AIC/BIC.

Q4. How do you test stationarity? ADF (Augmented Dickey–Fuller) test; difference until stationary.

Q5. ARIMA vs SARIMA? SARIMA adds seasonal AR/I/MA terms $(P,D,Q)_s$ for periodic patterns.

Q6. What if residuals aren't white noise? The model is under-specified — adjust $p/q$; check with the Ljung–Box test.

Q7. ARIMA vs Prophet vs LSTM? ARIMA = linear classical (small data, interpretable); Prophet = trend+seasonality+holidays; LSTM/TFT = non-linear, needs lots of data.

Q8. What does differencing do to forecasts? You forecast the differenced series then integrate (cumulative-sum) back to the original scale.

ARIMA(p,d,q) forecasts a time series from its own past by combining autoregression (p lagged values), differencing (d times, to enforce stationarity), and a moving average of the last q forecast errors. Gradient boosting that handles categorical features natively and fixes a subtle leakage bug in boosting via ordered boosting — strong accuracy with almost no preprocessing.

1. Definition

categorical dataordered target statisticsordered boostingsymmetric trees

CatBoost is a gradient-boosting library specialized for categorical data. It encodes categories with ordered target statistics (a leakage-safe target encoding) and trains with ordered boosting plus symmetric trees, giving excellent results with minimal tuning and no manual one-hot encoding.

•

2. Intuition & how it works

leaksprior rows in a random permutationPrediction shift in boostingOrdered boostingsymmetric/oblivious trees

Two problems CatBoost solves elegantly:

  1. Categorical encoding without leakage. Naive target encoding (replace a category with its mean target) leaks a row's own label into its feature. CatBoost computes the statistic using only prior rows in a random permutation, so a row never sees its own target.
  2. Prediction shift in boosting. Standard boosting computes gradients on the same data used to build the model, biasing them. Ordered boosting maintains models trained on prefixes of a permutation, so each row's gradient comes from a model that never saw it — less overfitting.

It also uses symmetric/oblivious trees (the same split condition across an entire level): strong regularization, extremely fast inference.

•

3. Mechanisms (what's distinctive)

Ordered target statisticbeforeOrdered boosting

Ordered target statistic for category $i$ (along a permutation, prior $p$, weight $a$):

$$\hat x_i=\frac{\sum_{j<i}[x_j=x_i]\,y_j + a\,p}{\sum_{j<i}[x_j=x_i] + a}$$

Only rows before $i$ contribute → no leakage; $a\cdot p$ is smoothing for rare categories. Ordered boosting: multiple permutations; each sample's gradient estimated from a model trained on earlier samples only. CatBoost also builds feature combinations of categoricals automatically.

•

4. Assumptions

Tree-based → no statistical assumptions (no scaling). Implicit: shallow symmetric trees, reasonable learning rate. Designed for datasets with many categorical features.

•

1. Definition

densely packednoise

DBSCAN groups points that are densely packed (many neighbors within radius eps) and marks points in low-density regions as noise. A cluster is a maximal set of density-connected points. It needs no number of clusters and finds non-convex shapes.

2. Intuition & how it works

≥ minPtsdensity-reachablebordernoise

Walk the dataset: a point with ≥ minPts neighbors inside radius ε is a core point — it sits in a dense region. Start a cluster there and keep absorbing all points density-reachable from it (chain through neighboring core points). Points reachable from a core but not themselves core are border points. Points reachable from nothing are noise.

3. Mathematical formulation

ε-neighborhoodCore pointDirectly density-reachableDensity-reachableDensity-connectedcore
  • ε-neighborhood: $N_\varepsilon(p)={q : d(p,q)\le\varepsilon}$.
  • Core point: $|N_\varepsilon(p)|\ge\text{minPts}$.
  • Directly density-reachable: $q$ from $p$ if $p$ is core and $q\in N_\varepsilon(p)$.
  • Density-reachable: a chain of directly-reachable core points.
  • Density-connected: $p,q$ both density-reachable from some core $o$ → same cluster.

Worked example (1-D, ε=1.5, minPts=3). Points ${1,2,2.5,3,8,9,20}$. - 2 has neighbors {1,2,2.5,3} (4 ≥ 3) → core; 1,2.5,3 similarly dense → the block ${1,2,2.5,3}$ forms cluster 1. - 8 has neighbors {9} only (2 < 3) → not core; 9 likewise → both noise (or border if near a core — here not). 20 is isolated → noise. Result: one cluster ${1,2,2.5,3}$, noise ${8,9,20}$ — and we never specified k.

1. Definition

splitsclassvalue

A decision tree is a supervised model that recursively splits the feature space along one feature at a time, building a tree of decision rules. Prediction follows the rules from root to a leaf, which holds the predicted class (majority vote) or value (mean of the leaf's samples).

•

2. Intuition & how it works

most informative question firstpure

The tree asks the most informative question first, then recurses on each branch. "Most informative" = the split that makes the resulting child groups as pure as possible (each dominated by one class, or tightly clustered in value).

                 [tenure < 12 months?]
                  ╱yes              ╲no
        [monthly charge > $80?]     churn = No (95%)
         ╱yes          ╲no
   churn=Yes(80%)   churn=No(70%)

It grows greedily — pick the best split now, never reconsider — until a stopping rule (max depth, min samples, no further gain) halts growth. Then each leaf predicts from the training samples that landed in it.

•

3. How splitting works — ID3, C4.5 & CART (with worked numbers)

maximizes impurity decreaseall of themEntropyInformation GainID3 picks Windmultiway

At each node the tree picks the split that maximizes impurity decrease:

$$\Delta I = I(\text{parent}) - \sum_{k}\frac{n_k}{n}\,I(\text{child}_k)$$

The three classic algorithms differ in the impurity $I$, the split shape, and the feature types they handle. We compute all of them on one tiny dataset.

Dataset (6 rows): predict Play from Outlook and Wind.

#OutlookWindPlay
1SunnyWeakNo
2SunnyStrongNo
3SunnyWeakYes
4RainWeakYes
5RainStrongNo
6RainWeakYes

Target: 3 Yes / 3 No.

3.1 ID3 — entropy & information gain

Entropy (disorder, in bits): $H(S)=-\sum_c p_c\log_2 p_c$.

Parent: $p_{Yes}=p_{No}=0.5 \Rightarrow H(S)=-0.5\log_2 0.5-0.5\log_2 0.5 = 1.0$ bit.

Information Gain = parent entropy − weighted child entropy.

Split on Wind: - Weak {1,3,4,6} = {No,Yes,Yes,Yes} = 3Y/1N → $H=-0.75\log_2 0.75-0.25\log_2 0.25=0.811$ - Strong {2,5} = {No,No} → $H=0$

$$H_{child}=\tfrac46(0.811)+\tfrac26(0)=0.541 \;\Rightarrow\; IG(\text{Wind})=1.0-0.541=\mathbf{0.459}$$

Split on Outlook: Sunny {1,2,3}=1Y/2N → $H=0.918$; Rain {4,5,6}=2Y/1N → $H=0.918$.

$$H_{child}=0.918 \;\Rightarrow\; IG(\text{Outlook})=1.0-0.918=\mathbf{0.082}$$

ID3 picks Wind (0.459 > 0.082). ID3 uses multiway splits, categorical only, no pruning → overfits.

3.2 C4.5 — gain ratio (fixing ID3's bias)

Information gain is biased toward high-cardinality features (many values → trivially pure children). C4.5 normalizes by split information:

$$\text{GainRatio}=\frac{IG}{\text{SplitInfo}}, \qquad \text{SplitInfo}=-\sum_k\frac{n_k}{n}\log_2\frac{n_k}{n}$$

  • Wind: SplitInfo $=-\tfrac46\log_2\tfrac46-\tfrac26\log_2\tfrac26=0.918$ → GainRatio $=0.459/0.918=\mathbf{0.50}$
  • Outlook: SplitInfo $=1.0$ → GainRatio $=\mathbf{0.082}$

C4.5 also handles continuous features (threshold tests), missing values, and does post-pruning — ID3's practical successor.

3.3 CART — Gini impurity (classification)

CART uses Gini $G=1-\sum_c p_c^2$ and always makes binary splits.

Parent: $G(S)=1-(0.5^2+0.5^2)=0.5$.

Split on Wind: Weak (3Y/1N) $G=1-(0.75^2+0.25^2)=0.375$; Strong (0Y/2N) $G=0$.

$$G_{child}=\tfrac46(0.375)+\tfrac26(0)=0.25 \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta G=0.5-0.25=\mathbf{0.25}$$

Split on Outlook: both children $G=1-((1/3)^2+(2/3)^2)=0.444$ → $\Delta G=\mathbf{0.056}$.

CART also picks Wind. Gini ≈ entropy in practice; Gini is cheaper (no log) and is sklearn's default.

3.4 CART regression — variance (MSE) reduction

For continuous targets, impurity = variance (≡ SSE), and the leaf predicts the mean.

Data: Hours=[1,2,3,10,11,12], Score=[2,3,4,20,21,22]; mean=12.

Parent SSE $=\sum(y-12)^2=100+81+64+64+81+100=490$ (variance $=81.7$).

Split at Hours ≤ 3: Left {2,3,4} mean 3 → SSE=2; Right {20,21,22} mean 21 → SSE=2. Child SSE $=4$ → variance drops 490 → 4 (near-perfect). Leaves predict 3 and 21.

4. Continuous splits, pruning, importance

Continuous featuresbetween adjacent valuesCost-complexity pruning (CART)post-pruningpre-pruningFeature importance

Continuous features: sort the values, test thresholds between adjacent values, keep the one with maximum impurity decrease — $O(n\log n)$ per feature per node.

Cost-complexity pruning (CART). Grow a full tree, then collapse subtrees that don't pay their way. Minimize

$$R_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha\,|\tilde{T}|$$

where $R(T)$ is the tree's total leaf impurity/error, $|\tilde{T}|$ the number of leaves, and $\alpha\ge0$ the complexity penalty. Larger $\alpha$ → smaller tree. Choose $\alpha$ by cross-validation (cost_complexity_pruning_path → ccp_alpha). This is post-pruning; setting max_depth/min_samples_leaf is pre-pruning.

Feature importance = total (sample-weighted) impurity decrease contributed by each feature's splits, normalized to sum to 1. Fast but biased toward high-cardinality features — prefer permutation importance for honesty.

•

5. Assumptions

non-parametricno statistical assumptions

Decision trees are non-parametric — essentially no statistical assumptions:

  • ❌ no linearity, no feature-distribution assumption (normality etc.).
  • ❌ no homoscedasticity / independence-of-errors requirement.
  • ❌ no feature scaling needed (splits are threshold-based).

One implicit modeling assumption: the target is well approximated by axis-aligned, piecewise-constant regions. They also assume the training sample is representative (they fit it very closely).

Q1. Gini vs entropy? Both measure node impurity and give near-identical trees; Gini is cheaper (no log), entropy (info gain) is more theoretically grounded.

Q2. Why do trees overfit? Grown fully, each leaf can memorize a few points → zero training error, high variance. Depth limits / pruning / ensembles counter it.

Q3. How are continuous splits chosen? Sort values; test thresholds between adjacent values; pick the max impurity decrease.

Q4. Why are trees unstable? Greedy top-down choices mean a small change near the root cascades into a totally different tree — motivating bagging.

Q5. Pre- vs post-pruning? Pre = stop early (max_depth); post = grow full then collapse via ccp_alpha (usually better).

Q6. What does cost-complexity pruning minimize? $R_\alpha(T)=R(T)+\alpha|\tilde T|$ — error plus a penalty per leaf; larger $\alpha$ → smaller tree.

Q7. ID3 vs CART? ID3: entropy/info-gain, multiway, categorical, no pruning, no regression. CART: Gini/variance, binary splits, both feature types, regression, pruning — and it's what sklearn implements.

Q8. Why is information gain biased, and how does C4.5 fix it? It favors high-cardinality features (more values → purer children); C4.5 divides by split information (gain ratio) to penalize that.

Q9. How does a regression tree predict? Leaf value = mean of its training targets; splits minimize variance/SSE.

Q10. Are impurity-based importances reliable? Biased toward high-cardinality / continuous features; use permutation importance instead.

Q11. Do trees need feature scaling? No — splits are threshold comparisons, scale- invariant.

Q12. How are missing values handled? Surrogate splits (CART) or sending them down a default branch (some libraries).

Q13. Can a tree model interactions? Yes, automatically — a split conditions on the path of earlier splits.

Q14. Tree vs Random Forest vs GBM in one line? Tree = interpretable/high-variance; RF = average independent trees (↓variance); GBM = sequential error-correcting trees (↓bias).

Q15. Why is finding the optimal tree hard? Globally optimal tree induction is NP-hard → greedy heuristics.

1. Definition

Elastic Net minimizes RSS plus a weighted mix of the L1 and L2 penalties:

$$J(\beta)=\sum_i(y_i-\mathbf{x}_i^\top\beta)^2 + \lambda\Big(\alpha\sum_j|\beta_j| + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\sum_j\beta_j^2\Big)$$

$\lambda$ sets overall strength; $\alpha\in[0,1]$ (sklearn's l1_ratio) sets the mix.

•

2. Intuition & how it works

adds both penaltiessparsity / selectiongrouping effectWhy grouping happensequal

Lasso selects features but goes haywire when features are correlated (keeps one of a group at random). Ridge is stable with correlation but never zeros anything. Elastic Net adds both penalties, giving:

  • the sparsity / selection of L1, and
  • the grouping effect of L2 — correlated features are kept or dropped together (their coefficients shrink toward each other) instead of one being picked at random.

Why grouping happens: for a fixed sum, $\beta_1^2+\beta_2^2$ is minimized when the two coefficients are equal, so the L2 term rewards correlated twins for sharing the load rather than fighting over it.

•

3. Mathematical formulation

coordinate descentnumeratordenominatormore than $n$Worked intuition

The penalty is the convex combination of L1 and L2:

$$P(\beta)=\alpha\lVert\beta\rVert_1 + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2$$

No closed form (L1 is non-smooth) → coordinate descent, with a per-coordinate update that is soft-threshold (L1) then shrink (L2):

$$\hat\beta_j=\frac{\operatorname{sign}(z_j)\,(|z_j|-\lambda\alpha)_+}{1+\lambda(1-\alpha)}$$

The numerator is Lasso's soft-threshold (creates zeros); the denominator is Ridge's proportional shrinkage (adds stability). Unlike Lasso, Elastic Net can select more than $n$ features when $p>n$.

Worked intuition. With $z_j=0.9,\ \lambda=1,\ \alpha=0.5$: numerator $=\text{sign}(0.9)(0.9-0.5)+=0.4$, denominator $=1+0.5=1.5$ → $\hat\beta_j=0.27$. A smaller signal $z_j=0.4$ → numerator $(0.4-0.5)+=0$ → $\hat\beta_j=0$ (dropped). So it still zeros weak features, but shrinks survivors more gently than pure Lasso.

Special cases: $\alpha=1$ → Lasso; $\alpha=0$ → Ridge.

1. Definition

linear predictorlink functionexponential-family

A GLM generalizes linear regression to response variables whose errors are not Gaussian (counts, binary, positive skewed, rates). It keeps a linear predictor $\eta=X\beta$ but connects it to the mean of the response through a link function $g$, and assumes the response follows an exponential-family distribution.

•

2. Intuition & how it works

countlinkthe same model

Ordinary linear regression assumes a continuous, Normal target — but a count (number of website visits, defects, claims) is a non-negative integer with variance that grows with the mean. Forcing linear regression on it can predict negative counts and gives wrong error bars. A GLM fixes this:

  • pick a distribution that matches the target (Poisson for counts),
  • pick a link that maps the mean to the whole real line (log for counts, so $\mu=e^{X\beta}>0$ always),
  • regress on that scale.

Linear, logistic, and Poisson regression are then the same model with different distribution + link choices.

•

3. Mathematical formulation

three componentsRandom componentSystematic componentLink functionLinearLogistic

A GLM has three components:

  1. Random component: $y$ follows an exponential-family distribution (Normal, Bernoulli, Poisson, Gamma, …) with mean $\mu=\mathbb E[y]$.
  2. Systematic component: the linear predictor $\eta=\mathbf{x}^\top\beta$.
  3. Link function: $g(\mu)=\eta$, i.e. $\mu=g^{-1}(\mathbf{x}^\top\beta)$.

Poisson regression (the canonical count GLM): $\ln\mu=\mathbf{x}^\top\beta\Rightarrow \mu=e^{\mathbf{x}^\top\beta}$. Fit by maximizing the Poisson log-likelihood $\sum_i(y_i\ln\mu_i-\mu_i)$ — no closed form, solved by IRLS (Newton steps that look like weighted least squares).

Coefficient interpretation: $e^{\beta_j}$ is a rate ratio — a one-unit increase in $x_j$ multiplies the expected count by $e^{\beta_j}$. Worked: $\beta_j=0.4\Rightarrow e^{0.4}=1.49$: each unit of $x_j$ raises the expected count by ~49%. An offset $\ln(\text{exposure})$ models rates (events per unit time/population).

1. Definition

sequentiallynegative gradient of the lossresidual errorssum

Gradient Boosting builds an ensemble sequentially: each new weak learner (a shallow tree) predicts the negative gradient of the loss w.r.t. the current model's predictions — i.e. it fits the residual errors. Predictions are the sum of all trees, each scaled by a learning rate.

$$F_m(\mathbf{x}) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \nu\,h_m(\mathbf{x})$$

•

2. Intuition & how it works

Start with a constant (the mean). Look at where you're wrong (residuals), train a small tree to predict those residuals, add a fraction $\nu$ of it. Now you're a little less wrong. Repeat hundreds of times — each tree nudges predictions toward truth.

Worked intuition (squared loss). True $y=[10,20,30]$; start $F_0=20$ (mean). Residuals $[-10,0,+10]$. Tree 1 fits them; add $\nu=0.1$ → predictions move to $[19,20,21]$. New residuals $[-9,0,+9]$ shrink; tree 2 fits those; etc. For squared loss the negative gradient is the residual $y-F$ — that's why "fit the residuals" works.

•

3. Mathematical formulation

pseudo-residualsSquared lossLog loss

Boosting is forward stagewise additive modeling. At stage $m$, compute the pseudo-residuals (negative gradient of loss $L$ at the current model):

$$r_{im}=-\left[\frac{\partial L(y_i, F(\mathbf{x}i))}{\partial F(\mathbf{x}_i)}\right]{F=F_{m-1}}$$

Fit a tree $h_m$ to the $r_{im}$, set leaf values that minimize the loss, then update $F_m=F_{m-1}+\nu h_m$ with $0<\nu\le1$.

  • Squared loss → pseudo-residual = ordinary residual $y-F$.
  • Log loss (classification) → pseudo-residual = $y-p$ (label − predicted probability).
  • Swapping the loss (Huber, quantile, …) is what makes it general.

Learning-rate / n_estimators trade-off: small $\nu$ + many trees generalizes best (shrinkage) but costs more compute → tune together, use early stopping.

•

4. Assumptions

Tree-based → no statistical assumptions (no scaling/distribution/linearity). Implicit: weak/shallow trees (depth 3–8), small learning rate, data not too noisy.

1. Definition

Hierarchical clustering produces a hierarchy of clusters shown as a dendrogram. The common agglomerative (bottom-up) variant starts with each point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until one remains; divisive does the reverse. Cut the dendrogram at a height to get a flat clustering.

2. Intuition & how it works

heightcut horizontally

Merge the two nearest groups, over and over, recording each merge. The result is a tree: leaves are points, the root is everything. The height of a merge encodes how dissimilar the merged clusters were.

Q4. Why doesn't it scale? Needs the full $n\times n$ distance matrix → $O(n^2)$ memory.

Q5. Hierarchical vs K-Means? No preset k and a full hierarchy, but $O(n^2)$ cost and greedy/irreversible merges; K-Means is cheap but needs k and assumes round clusters.

Q6. What is chaining? Single linkage can link far clusters through a bridge of close points, producing straggly clusters.

Q7. Can it assign new points? Not natively — you'd recompute or use a different model.

1. Definition

Anomaliesfew splits

Isolation Forest builds many random binary trees ("iTrees") by repeatedly picking a random feature and a random split value. Anomalies — being few and different — get separated from the rest in few splits (short path from root to leaf), so the average path length across trees is the anomaly score.

•

2. Intuition & how it works

manya few

To isolate a point, keep splitting the data randomly until that point is alone. A point in a dense cluster needs many splits to peel away from its neighbors; an outlier sitting off on its own gets isolated in a few. Short average path = anomaly.

normal point (deep leaf)        anomaly (shallow leaf)
  o─o─o─o─o─o─o (many splits)      o─o (isolated fast)
•

3. Mathematical formulation

anomaly scoreanomalysubsampling

For a point $x$, let $h(x)$ be its path length in an iTree. Average over the forest, then normalize by $c(n)$, the average path length of an unsuccessful BST search on $n$ points ($c(n)\approx 2\ln(n-1)-2(n-1)/n$). The anomaly score:

$$s(x,n)=2^{-\frac{\mathbb E[h(x)]}{c(n)}}$$

  • $s\to1$ → short paths → anomaly.
  • $s\ll0.5$ → long paths → normal.
  • $s\approx0.5$ → no clear anomaly.

Trees use subsampling (e.g. 256 points) — small samples actually improve isolation of anomalies and keep it fast.

1. Definition

K-Means partitions $n$ points into $k$ clusters to minimize the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) — the total squared distance from each point to its cluster centroid:

$$\text{WCSS}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in C_j}\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mu_j\rVert^2$$

•

2. Intuition & how it works (Lloyd's algorithm)

AssignUpdateWorked iterationconverged

Drop $k$ centroids, then alternate two steps until nothing changes:

  1. Assign: each point joins its nearest centroid.
  2. Update: each centroid moves to the mean of its assigned points.
init        assign         update         converged
 ×  ·        ×→a ·→a        a,b move        stable
   ·  ×        ·→b ×→b      to means of    clusters
 ·    ·      ·→a   ·→b      their points

Worked iteration. 1-D points ${1,2,10,11}$, $k=2$, init $\mu_1=1,\mu_2=2$. - Assign: 1→μ1; 2,10,11→μ2. Update: μ1=1, μ2=mean(2,10,11)=7.67. - Assign: 1,2→μ1; 10,11→μ2. Update: μ1=1.5, μ2=10.5. Assign again: unchanged → converged. Clusters ${1,2},{10,11}$, WCSS $=(0.5^2{+}0.5^2){+}(0.5^2{+}0.5^2)=1.0$.

•

3. Mathematical formulation

non-convexlocalmeank-means++ initializationChoosing kElbow method

K-Means greedily minimizes WCSS; the objective is non-convex, so it converges to a local optimum dependent on initialization. The two Lloyd steps are exactly coordinate descent on WCSS: the mean is the point minimizing squared distance to a group (hence "centroid = mean").

k-means++ initialization. Choose the first centroid uniformly at random; choose each next centroid with probability $\propto D(\mathbf{x})^2$, the squared distance to the nearest already-chosen centroid. This spreads seeds out → far better, more stable solutions (sklearn default).

Choosing k: - Elbow method — plot WCSS vs $k$; pick the "bend" where adding clusters stops helping much. - Silhouette $s(i)=\dfrac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max(a(i),b(i))}\in[-1,1]$, where $a(i)$ = mean intra-cluster distance, $b(i)$ = mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Higher is better.

Choosing a clustering model & selecting k (beyond the elbow)

Interview: "For a churn dataset, which model would you use to segment customers, and how do you pick k without the elbow graph?"

Which clustering model for customer segmentation?

  • K-Meansthe default for segmentation: fast, scales to 1M+ rows, compact clusters that are easy to explain to the business ("high-value, low-tenure"). Needs scaled features and a chosen k; assumes roughly spherical, similar-size clusters.
  • K-Prototypes / K-Modeswhen data is mixed numeric + categorical (plan, region) — plain K-Means can't handle categoricals.
  • Gaussian Mixture (GMM)soft assignments + elliptical clusters (a customer can be 70% segment A); use when clusters overlap or vary in shape; choose components by BIC/AIC.
  • Hierarchicalgives a dendrogram / segment hierarchy when you don't know k; doesn't scale to 1M (run on a sample).
  • DBSCAN / HDBSCANirregular segments + automatic outlier/noise labeling; struggles with varying density and high dimensions.

For a 1M×200 churn table: scale → reduce dimensions (PCA) → K-Means (or K-Prototypes for mixed types) as the workhorse, validate with the indices below, then profile each segment for business meaning.

Selecting k — methods other than the elbow

The elbow (inertia/WCSS vs k) is eyeballed and often ambiguous. Quantitative alternatives: - Silhouette score — per point (b−a)/max(a,b) (a=mean intra-cluster dist, b=mean nearest-other-cluster dist), range −1…1; pick the k with the highest mean silhouette. The most common elbow replacement. - Gap statistic — compares within-cluster dispersion to that expected under a uniform "no-cluster" reference (Monte-Carlo); pick the smallest k whose gap is within 1 SE of the next — a principled stopping rule. - Davies–Bouldin index — avg (within-cluster scatter ÷ between-cluster separation); lower is better → minimize over k. - Calinski–Harabasz (Variance Ratio) — between-cluster ÷ within-cluster dispersion; higher is better → maximize. - Dunn index — min inter-cluster distance ÷ max intra-cluster diameter; higher is better (well-separated, compact clusters); conceptually clean but sensitive to noise. - BIC / AIC (for GMM) — information criteria balancing fit vs #parameters; pick the lowest. - Stability / consensus — re-cluster on bootstraps; the k giving the most stable assignments is more trustworthy. - Business validity — the ultimate test: are segments distinct, sizeable, and actionable? A statistically "best" k with unusable segments is wrong.

Practice: compute Silhouette + Davies–Bouldin + Calinski–Harabasz across a range of k, look for agreement, then sanity-check the segment profiles.

1. Definition

majority voteaverage

KNN predicts the label of a new point by finding the k training points closest to it (by a distance metric) and taking a majority vote (classification) or average (regression) of their targets. There is no model to train — the model is the dataset.

•

2. Intuition & how it works

high variancehigh bias

To classify a new fruit as apple or orange, look at the k most similar fruits you've seen and go with the majority. "Similar" = small distance in feature space.

      ×  ×            ? = new point (query)
   ×   (?)  ○         circle = its k nearest neighbors
        ○  ○          → vote among the circled points
  • Small k → flexible, jagged boundary (low bias, high variance, noise-sensitive).
  • Large k → smooth boundary (high bias, low variance); at $k=n$ it predicts the global majority.
•

3. Mathematical formulation

EuclideanManhattanCosineDistance-weightedk=3Red

3.1 Distance metrics (Minkowski family)

$$d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \Big(\sum_{j=1}^{p}|x_j-x'_j|^q\Big)^{1/q}$$

  • $q=2$ → Euclidean (straight-line); $q=1$ → Manhattan (grid); $q\to\infty$ → Chebyshev (max coordinate diff). Cosine distance for text/embeddings (direction, not magnitude).

3.2 Prediction rule

Let $N_k(\mathbf{x})$ be the indices of the k nearest neighbors:

$$\hat{y}=\text{mode}{y_i: i\in N_k}\ \text{(classification)}, \qquad \hat{y}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in N_k}y_i\ \text{(regression)}$$

Distance-weighted variant: weight each neighbor's vote by $w_i=1/d_i$ (or a Gaussian kernel) so closer neighbors count more.

  • distance-weighted (k=3): Red weight $=1/1.00+1/2.24=1.45$, Blue $=1/2.83=0.35$ → Red (confidently). The vote sharpens when closer points count more.

3.4 The curse of dimensionality (with numbers)

KNN relies on "nearby" being meaningful, which breaks in high dimensions.

  • Consider a unit hypercube. The fraction of its volume within a thin shell of thickness 0.1 from the surface is $1-0.9^{d}$: d=1 → 10%, d=10 → 65%, d=100 → 99.997%. Almost all points sit near the boundary, far from each other.
  • The ratio $\frac{d_{\max}-d_{\min}}{d_{\min}}\to 0$ as $d\to\infty$ — the nearest and farthest neighbors become equidistant, so "nearest" loses meaning.

Lesson: reduce dimensionality (PCA/embeddings) before KNN, or use a model that isn't distance-based.

Q1. Is KNN parametric? No — non-parametric and instance-based; "training" just stores the data.

Q2. How do you choose k? Cross-validation; rule of thumb $k\approx\sqrt{n}$; use odd k for binary classification to avoid ties.

Q3. Bias–variance with k? Small k = low bias / high variance; large k = high bias / low variance.

Q4. Why scale features? Distance is scale-sensitive; unscaled large-range features dominate the metric.

Q5. Why does KNN suffer in high dimensions? The ratio of nearest to farthest distance → 1, so "nearest" loses meaning; almost all volume is near the boundary.

Q6. Euclidean vs Manhattan vs cosine — when? Euclidean for continuous comparable features; Manhattan when dimensions are independent / for robustness; cosine for text/embeddings where direction matters.

Q7. How to speed it up? KD-tree/Ball-tree (low-mid dims), or approximate NN (HNSW/FAISS) for high dims / large n.

Q8. Lazy vs eager learning? KNN is lazy (defers all computation to query time); logistic/trees are eager (build a model upfront).

Q9. Distance-weighted KNN? Weight neighbors by $1/d$ so closer points have more influence — smooths and reduces ties.

Q10. KNN for regression? Average (or distance-weighted average) of the neighbors' target values.

Q11. Effect of irrelevant features? They add noise to the distance and degrade accuracy — KNN has no built-in feature weighting.

Q12. How does KNN handle ties (binary, even k)? Avoid with odd k, or break by nearest distance / distance weighting.

13. Common mistakes & best practices

  • ❌ Not scaling features — the #1 KNN mistake.
  • ❌ KNN on high-dimensional raw data without reduction.
  • ❌ Even k in binary classification (ties).
  • ✅ Standardize inside a pipeline (prevents leakage).
  • ✅ Tune k; try weights="distance".
  • ✅ Use an ANN index for large-scale similarity search.

14. One-paragraph summary

feature scaling is mandatoryRelated

KNN is a lazy, non-parametric learner that classifies (votes) or regresses (averages) using the k closest training points under a distance metric. It assumes local smoothness and meaningful distances, so feature scaling is mandatory and high-dimensional data wrecks it (curse of dimensionality — nearest≈farthest). With no training cost but $O(nd)$ inference, it shines on small, low-dimensional, irregular problems and for similarity search (use KD/Ball-trees or approximate NN to scale), but loses to compact parametric models when dimensionality, data size, or interpretability matter. Tune k (bias–variance) and consider distance weighting.
